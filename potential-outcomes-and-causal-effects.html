Causal Inference in Epidemiology Using R

Chapter 3 Potential outcomes and causal effects

Chapter 1 asked whether Cox-2 selective NSAIDs cause less peptic ulcer disease than nonselective NSAIDs, and then showed that the obvious way of answering the question — comparing crude risks between the two groups — does not actually answer it, because Cox-2 initiators and nonselective initiators differ in ways connected to ulcer risk before either drug is ever taken. This chapter builds the vocabulary that makes precise what went wrong, and what a right answer would even mean. The framework is called potential outcomes, and once you have it, “causal effect” stops being a vague intuition and becomes something you can write down, simulate, and eventually estimate.

3.1 Two futures for every patient

Picture a single patient in the ns cohort — call her Patient 47 — who shows up at her physician’s office with joint pain that warrants an NSAID. Her physician has a choice: prescribe a nonselective NSAID, or prescribe a Cox-2 selective inhibitor. Whichever one is chosen, Patient 47 either does or does not go on to develop peptic ulcer disease over the following year. But the question “would she have developed PUD if she had been treated differently” has a sensible answer even though it did not happen. If she was in fact given the Cox-2 drug and stayed ulcer-free, we can still ask, coherently, what would have happened to her, with everything else about her held fixed, if she had instead received the nonselective drug.

Potential outcomes give that question a name and a symbol. Let \(A\) denote the treatment Patient 47 receives, coded \(A = 1\) for the Cox-2 selective drug and \(A = 0\) for the nonselective drug, and let \(Y\) denote whether she develops incident PUD. Then \(Y^1\) is the outcome she would have if she were treated with \(A = 1\), and \(Y^0\) is the outcome she would have if she were treated with \(A = 0\). These are potential outcomes — one for each treatment value she could in principle receive — and both exist, in the sense of being well-defined quantities, before we know which treatment she actually gets. Once treatment is assigned, one of them becomes the observed outcome and the other becomes counterfactual: contrary to the fact of what happened. If Patient 47 actually receives the Cox-2 drug, we observe \(Y^1\); \(Y^0\) is what her chart would show in the world where her physician wrote the other prescription.

A word on notation, because it varies more than the idea does. We write these potential outcomes with superscripts, but many authors — and the PHS 921 lectures and exams — write \(Y(1)\) and \(Y(0)\), and others write \(Y_a\) for the outcome under a generic treatment value \(a\). All three notations denote the same objects, so it is worth learning to move between them freely rather than attaching the concept to any one of them.

With both potential outcomes in hand for a single patient, her individual causal effect of Cox-2 selectivity on PUD is simply the contrast between them, \(Y^1 - Y^0\). If \(Y^1 = 0\) and \(Y^0 = 1\), the Cox-2 drug prevented her ulcer; if both are 0, or both are 1, the choice of drug made no difference to her outcome, even though the drugs may differ enormously for other patients. This is a clean, well-defined quantity — and it is also, for any real patient, unobservable. We only ever see one of Patient 47’s two potential outcomes, because she can only actually take one drug. This is what Holland (1986) called the fundamental problem of causal inference: for any individual, at most one potential outcome is ever realized, and the individual causal effect can never be computed from data on that individual alone, no matter how much we know about her or how long we follow her. Every method in this book is, at bottom, a strategy for getting around this problem — not by recovering the missing potential outcome for any one patient, but by using groups of patients to estimate causal effects that are well defined on average, even though they are unknowable one person at a time.

3.2 A god’s-eye view: simulation

The fundamental problem of causal inference is a genuine, permanent limitation of real data: no data set on real patients ever contains both of anyone’s potential outcomes. But nothing stops us from simulating a toy population in which we, as the people writing the code, get to decide both potential outcomes for every patient, and then watch what happens when only one becomes observed. This is a god’s-eye view of exactly the causal question Chapter 1 raised, and it lets us see confounding happen mechanically, in code, rather than only argue about it in prose.

Imagine 100,000 patients with a severity indicator, severity, that plays the role of an unmeasured driver of both treatment and outcome — capturing whatever led some patients toward the Cox-2 drug and also independently raised their ulcer risk. For each patient we generate both potential outcomes, y0 (the outcome under nonselective NSAID) and y1 (the outcome under Cox-2 selective drug), with the Cox-2 potential outcome built to carry a lower baseline risk — the drug is genuinely protective in this toy world. We then assign actual treatment a so that more severe patients are more likely to receive the Cox-2 drug, exactly the channeling Chapter 1 described, and finally construct the single outcome y actually recorded: the potential outcome matching the treatment each patient actually got.

set.seed(921)
truth <- tibble(
  severity = rbinom(100000, 1, 0.3),                       # confounder
  y0 = rbinom(100000, 1, 0.05 + 0.15 * severity),          # risk untreated
  y1 = rbinom(100000, 1, 0.03 + 0.15 * severity),          # risk treated
  a  = rbinom(100000, 1, 0.2 + 0.5 * severity),            # treated more if severe
  y  = if_else(a == 1, y1, y0)                             # consistency
)
truth |> summarise(
  ate   = mean(y1) - mean(y0),
  att   = mean(y1[a == 1]) - mean(y0[a == 1]),
  naive = mean(y[a == 1]) - mean(y[a == 0])
)
## # A tibble: 1 x 3
##       ate     att  naive
##     <dbl>   <dbl>  <dbl>
## 1 -0.0202 -0.0237 0.0484

Because we generated y0 and y1 ourselves, we can compute the true average causal effect directly from columns no real data set would ever have both of at once: mean(y1) - mean(y0) is -0.020, a negative number, confirming that treatment is, on average, beneficial in this toy population — it lowers the risk of the outcome by about 2.0 percentage points. But the last line of the summary, naive, is the only contrast we could ever compute from a real observational data set — the mean of the observed outcome y among the treated minus the mean of y among the untreated — and it comes out to 0.048, positive and on the wrong side of zero. The naive comparison says Cox-2 selectivity looks harmful; the truth we built into the simulation is that it is helpful. The gap between 0.048 and -0.020 is confounding by severity, laid completely bare because in this one artificial world we happen to know the whole truth: severe patients were both more likely to be treated and more likely to have a bad outcome regardless of treatment, so the treated group looks worse than it would have if severity had been balanced across the two groups. Real data never hands you y0 and y1 side by side like this — but the machinery in the rest of the chapter exists precisely to close that gap without needing to.

3.3 Average causal effects and estimands

The individual causal effect \(Y^1 - Y^0\) cannot be observed, but its average over a population can be, once we have the right tools — which is why causal inference works with population-level summaries called estimands. The one that shows up most often in this book is the average treatment effect (ATE), \[\text{ATE} = E[Y^1] - E[Y^0],\] the average, over everyone in the population of interest, of what would happen if everyone were treated minus what would happen if no one were. In the simulation above, mean(y1) - mean(y0) computed over all 100,000 patients is the ATE, because we happened to have both potential outcomes for every one of them.

A second estimand, the average treatment effect in the treated (ATT), \[\text{ATT} = E[Y^1 \mid A = 1] - E[Y^0 \mid A = 1],\] restricts that same contrast to the patients who were actually treated: it asks what treatment did for the people who received it, not for the population as a whole. In the simulation, that is mean(y1[a == 1]) - mean(y0[a == 1]), and in our output it comes out very close to, but not exactly equal to, the ATE. That small remaining gap is Monte Carlo noise, not confounding: in this particular simulation the treatment effect y1 - y0 is built to be the same constant regardless of severity, so the true ATT equals the true ATE exactly, and the two only look different because both are estimated from a single finite sample of 100,000 patients rather than computed on the population itself. Rerunning with a different seed would shift the gap around, and often shrink it, without ever making it disappear completely.

The reason the two estimands can genuinely diverge, rather than merely look different by chance, is when the effect itself varies with whatever determines who gets treated. Which estimand you want then depends on the question. If a health system is deciding whether to switch every future NSAID prescription for arthritis patients to the Cox-2 selective drug, the ATE is the relevant quantity — it answers “what would happen if we treated everyone.” If a clinician is instead trying to understand the effect of the prescriptions she has already written, on the patients who actually received them, the ATT is closer to what she wants — it answers “was this decision, for the patients who got it, a good one.” The two estimands coincide only when treatment effects do not vary with whatever determines who gets treated, exactly the case built into this simulation; Exercise 3 asks you to break that by making the effect depend on severity, so that the ATE and ATT genuinely part ways.

Any of these estimands can be reported on more than one scale. With \(Y\) binary, as PUD is, the most direct scale is the risk difference, \(E[Y^1] - E[Y^0]\), interpretable as “how many fewer (or more) cases per 100 patients treated.” The risk ratio, \(E[Y^1] / E[Y^0]\), expresses the same contrast multiplicatively — a risk ratio of 0.7 means treatment reduces risk by 30%, regardless of baseline risk, which often makes it more portable across populations than a difference. The odds ratio, \[\frac{E[Y^1]/(1-E[Y^1])}{E[Y^0]/(1-E[Y^0])},\] is the scale most familiar from logistic regression and case-control studies, but it approximates the risk ratio only when the outcome is rare, and can otherwise diverge from it substantially. None of consistency, exchangeability, or positivity — the identification conditions we turn to next — depend on which scale you eventually report; the choice of scale is a separate decision, made after identification, about how to communicate a causal contrast you have already secured.

3.4 The identification conditions

Estimands like the ATE are defined in terms of potential outcomes we never fully observe. Identification is the step of showing that, under stated assumptions, an estimand written in potential outcomes is equal to some quantity we actually could compute from observed data. Three conditions do this work, and it is worth attaching each one to the NSAID cohort so that they read as claims about real patients rather than abstract axioms.

Consistency says that a patient’s observed outcome equals the potential outcome under the treatment she actually received: if Patient 47 takes the Cox-2 drug, her observed \(Y\) equals \(Y^1\), not some average or blend involving \(Y^0\). This sounds almost too obvious to state, but it is doing real work — it is what licenses the line y = if_else(a == 1, y1, y0) in the simulation above, and it can fail in practice when “treatment” is not well defined enough to pin down a single potential outcome. If “Cox-2 initiation” secretly bundles several different doses, durations, or concurrent gastroprotective strategies, then \(Y^1\) is not one well-defined quantity but several, and consistency is violated before any statistical issue even arises.

Exchangeability (also called ignorability) says that the treated and untreated groups would have had the same average outcome had they received the same treatment — formally, \(Y^1, Y^0 \perp A\), potential outcomes are statistically independent of the treatment actually received. This is exactly what fails in our simulation: because a depends on severity, and severity also drives y0 and y1, treated and untreated patients are not exchangeable, and that is the entire reason the naive contrast diverged from the ATE. Exchangeability is what a randomized trial buys you by construction — Chapter 4 makes that precise — and it is what confounding by indication takes away in observational data like ns, where physicians route sicker-looking patients toward the Cox-2 drug for reasons connected to the very outcome we are studying.

Full exchangeability is usually too much to hope for in observational data, which is why most of this book works with a weaker version, conditional exchangeability: \(Y^1, Y^0 \perp A \mid L\) for some set of measured covariates \(L\). Treatment need not be independent of potential outcomes overall, only within levels of \(L\) — among patients who share the same measured severity, say, treatment assignment is as good as random. This is a much more plausible assumption whenever we can measure the variables driving treatment choice, and it is exactly what standardization, propensity scores, and IPTW in later chapters are built to exploit.

We can see conditional exchangeability rescue our toy example directly, because we happen to have measured the one variable, severity, that was confounding the naive comparison. If we compute the same treated- minus-untreated contrast separately within each level of severity, using group_by(severity), we are conditioning on the confounder exactly the way conditional exchangeability requires:

by_severity <- truth |>
  group_by(severity) |>
  summarise(
    naive_within = mean(y[a == 1]) - mean(y[a == 0]),
    .groups = "drop"
  )
by_severity
## # A tibble: 2 x 2
##   severity naive_within
##      <int>        <dbl>
## 1        0      -0.0184
## 2        1      -0.0220

Within each level of severity, the treated-versus-untreated contrast comes out close to -0.020 — the true ATE — rather than the badly biased 0.048 we got by ignoring severity altogether. Averaging these two severity-specific contrasts back together, weighted by how many patients fall in each severity stratum, recovers -0.019, essentially the ATE. Nothing about the data changed; we only stopped comparing all treated patients to all untreated patients, and started comparing treated to untreated patients within the variable that was confounding the comparison. This hand-computed stratification is the seed of standardization, which Chapter 7 develops into a general tool for covariate sets far too large to stratify by hand.

Positivity (or overlap) says that every combination of covariates that occurs in the population has a nonzero probability of receiving either treatment level: \(0 < P(A = 1 \mid L = l) < 1\) for every value \(l\) of \(L\) with positive probability. This is a logical precondition, not a statistical nicety — if every patient with severity == 1 in our simulation had probability zero of getting the nonselective drug, there would be no untreated severe patients to compare to, and no amount of adjustment could recover what their outcome under nonselective treatment would have been. Positivity can fail structurally, when a covariate combination makes one treatment clinically unthinkable, or only practically, when the combination is simply rare in a finite sample; the second, less absolute failure is common enough in real cohorts like ns that later chapters return to it when diagnosing propensity score overlap.

Together, consistency, exchangeability (or its conditional version), and positivity are what let us write an equals sign between a causal estimand defined on unobservable potential outcomes and a statistical quantity computable from an observed data set like ns. Every adjustment method in the rest of this book — standardization, propensity scores, weighting — is a different computational strategy for the same identified quantity; none of them can substitute for these three conditions actually holding.

3.5 Exercises

  1. Modify the sim-po simulation so that the Cox-2 drug is harmful rather than beneficial — for instance, swap the coefficients so y1 has a higher baseline risk than y0 — and recompute the ATE, ATT, and naive contrast. Does the naive estimate still point in the wrong direction, and if so, which way?
  2. Starting from the original simulation, change how a is generated so that treatment no longer depends on severity at all (for example, rbinom(100000, 1, 0.4), a fixed probability for everyone). Recompute the three summary statistics. What happens to the gap between the naive contrast and the ATE, and why does that make sense in light of the exchangeability condition?
  3. Modify y1 so that the treatment effect itself differs by severity — for example, make the drug strongly protective for severe patients but do nothing for non-severe patients. Recompute the ATE and the ATT. Are they still close to each other? Relate what you find to the discussion of when ATE and ATT coincide, and to which estimand a clinician deciding whether to keep prescribing the drug to her current, already-treated patients should care about most.
