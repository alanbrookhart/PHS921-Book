Chapter 11 Inverse probability of censoring weights

Chapter 10 closed with a diagram that placed loss to follow-up between treatment and an unmeasured element of prognosis, making every risk set past time zero a selected sample and every Kaplan–Meier factor a comparison among survivors of that selection. It also promised that we already have the tool for the problem. Chapter 9 encountered a problem of the same shape, namely a variable associated with treatment that we could not condition on without introducing bias elsewhere, and addressed it by reweighting rather than conditioning. This chapter builds the corresponding weights for censoring, which requires reshaping the cohort into a form we will use for the rest of Part III.

11.1 Weighting returns

The two estimators apply the same idea to different columns. An inverse probability of treatment weight is the reciprocal of the probability of the treatment a patient actually received, \(1/P(A \mid L)\), and it builds a pseudo-population in which treatment is unrelated to \(L\). An inverse probability of censoring weight is the reciprocal of the probability of the observation status a patient actually had: among patients still under observation at time \(t\), each is weighted by one over her probability of having remained uncensored that long. She then stands in for herself plus the patients like her who dropped out, and in the reweighted data the people we can still see represent the people we started with.

One structural difference matters. Treatment here happens once, at time zero, so its weight is a single number per patient. Censoring accumulates: a patient must survive being censored in the first year, and then the second, and so on, and the chance of getting through all of it is a product of the chances of getting through each piece. Splitting follow-up into intervals \(k = 1, \dots, K\) and writing \(C_k = 1\) for censoring during interval \(k\), the weight in effect at interval \(t\) is

\[W^{C}(t) = \prod_{k \le t} \frac{1}{\Pr(C_k = 0 \mid C_{k-1} = 0, A, L)},\]

and its stabilized version replaces the 1 in each numerator with \(\Pr(C_k = 0 \mid C_{k-1} = 0)\), a probability that depends on time but not on covariates. As in Chapter 9 the numerator cannot undo the balancing the denominator achieves; it rescales, so that a stabilized weight averages near 1 and a mean far from 1 signals a misbehaving censoring model.

What the weights buy is a weaker but still unverifiable assumption. Kaplan–Meier requires censoring to be non-informative outright, whereas the weighted analysis requires it to be non-informative given the covariates in the censoring model, which is the survival-data version of conditional exchangeability. Two things are needed to fit it: a data structure with one row per patient per interval, and a model for censoring in that structure.

11.2 Person-period data

The step that makes everything else straightforward is to stop treating each patient as one row. Cut follow-up into yearly intervals and give a patient one row for each interval she survives into, carrying her baseline covariates along; the row records the interval, whether the outcome happened in it, and whether censoring happened in it. A patient followed 3.1 years contributes four rows, the last a stub from year 3 to year 3.1. survival::survSplit() does the cutting, and wrapping it fixes the column names we will rely on later.

make_person_period <- function(data, time, event, cuts) {
  data <- data |> mutate(.row_id = dplyr::row_number())
  survival::survSplit(
    as.formula(paste0("Surv(", time, ", ", event, ") ~ .")),
    data = data, cut = cuts, start = "tstart", end = "tstop",
    event = event
  ) |> as_tibble()
}

The . ~ on the right of the formula keeps every other column, so covariates are repeated down a patient’s rows unchanged. .row_id is added before splitting and is what glues the rows back into people — needed for the running product of weights and for the cluster-robust variance at the end. The event we split on is censoring, defined as being lost to follow-up before having a cardiovascular event; the next section takes up why that definition and not another. Because survSplit() cannot make an interval out of nothing, we first set aside the handful of patients whose follow-up ends at time zero.

library(survival)
source("R/ipcw.R")

sta_cens <- sta |>

pp <- make_person_period(sta_cens, time = "cens_time", event = "censored",
                         cuts = 1:9) |>
  mutate(event = as.integer(cv_event == 1 &
                            event_time > tstart & event_time <= tstop))

Two details of bookkeeping deserve naming, because getting either wrong silently changes the analysis. First, cens_time is a copy of event_time: survSplit() consumes the time variable it splits on and replaces it with tstart and tstop, so splitting on the copy leaves the original event_time intact as an ordinary carried-along column. Second, the outcome indicator has to be rebuilt by hand. The censored column that comes back from survSplit() is already interval-specific — 1 only in the interval where censoring happened — but cv_event is still the patient-level value, repeated down every row, so a patient with an event would appear to have one in each of her intervals. The event_time > tstart & event_time <= tstop comparison puts it in the one interval that contains it. Look at two patients, one lost to follow-up and one with an event, before and after expansion.

The rows read as a small clinical history: at risk through each interval, then one final interval in which either the outcome occurs or the patient leaves observation. Because the two indicators are now built from three different columns, they should be checked against the patient-level cohort rather than taken on trust.

Dropping the 8 patients with no follow-up leaves 6,157 patients and 44,485 person-period rows, about 7.2 intervals each. The expansion preserves 460 events — the same count as sum(sta$cv_event), since none of the dropped patients had one — and 2,837 censorings, matching the patient-level indicator exactly — both identities hold, which is TRUE. Checks as mundane as these are worth printing every time, because an event indicator that is correct at the patient level and wrong at the interval level produces plausible output and a wrong answer.

The reason to pay this price in rows is that a logistic regression fitted to person-period data estimates a discrete-time hazard. Each row asks whether the event happened in that interval among patients who reached it, which is exactly the conditional quantity Kaplan–Meier computes; a logistic model of that indicator, pooled over intervals and with time on the right-hand side, is a pooled logistic model and approximates a proportional hazards model when the interval hazards are small. Ours carries time as splines::ns(tstop, 3) rather than factor(tstop), spending three degrees of freedom instead of nine on a hazard we expect to be smooth. And because the hazards can be multiplied back up into a survival curve, the model returns risks at the horizons the target trial named, not only ratios.

11.3 Building censoring weights

The censoring indicator above makes a substantive choice worth defending. Of the ways follow-up ends in sta, only loss to follow-up is treated as the censoring we weight for. Administrative censoring at 10 years is excluded because it happens to everyone still present at a calendar boundary that no covariate predicts — the benign case of Chapter 10. Death is excluded for a less comfortable reason: it is a competing risk that Chapter 10 chose to treat as censoring, and putting it in the censoring model would weight the analysis toward a world in which nobody dies of anything else, an estimand harder to defend than the one we have. Both exclusions are substantive assumptions rather than conveniences, and the second is the weakest point in this chapter’s analysis.

With the indicator fixed, the censoring model is a pooled logistic regression of censored on time and the baseline covariates, fitted across all person-period rows at once. In lecture, and in much of the literature, censoring models are instead fit with Cox proportional hazards regression; the pooled logistic on person-period data is its discrete-time analog, and with intervals this short the two give essentially the same weights. Our estimator wraps the whole sequence: fit the censoring model, fit the numerator model, take the running product down each patient’s rows, truncate, and fit the weighted outcome model.

ipcw_estimator <- function(pp_data, censor_model, outcome_formula,
                           id = ".row_id", numerator_model = NULL,
                           truncate_at = 0.99) {
  cens_fit <- glm(censor_model, family = binomial(), data = pp_data)
  pp_data <- pp_data |> mutate(p_uncens = 1 - predict(cens_fit, type = "response"))

  if (!is.null(numerator_model)) {
    num_fit <- glm(numerator_model, family = binomial(), data = pp_data)
    pp_data <- pp_data |> mutate(p_uncens_num = 1 - predict(num_fit, type = "response"))
  } else {
    pp_data <- pp_data |> mutate(p_uncens_num = 1)
  }

  pp_data <- pp_data |>
    group_by(.data[[id]]) |>
    arrange(tstop, .by_group = TRUE) |>
    mutate(w_cens = cumprod(p_uncens_num) / cumprod(p_uncens)) |>
    ungroup() |>
    mutate(w_cens = pmin(w_cens, quantile(w_cens, truncate_at)))

  # geeglm() looks up `id` and `weights` in the environment of the formula it
  # is handed; ours was built by the caller, so re-home it here.
  environment(outcome_formula) <- environment()

  fit <- geepack::geeglm(
    outcome_formula, id = pp_data[[id]], weights = pp_data$w_cens,
    family = binomial(), data = pp_data
  )
  list(fit = broom::tidy(fit, conf.int = TRUE), data = pp_data)
}

The cumprod() line carries the time-varying idea in a single expression. Grouped by patient and ordered by interval, the weight at interval \(t\) is the running product of stabilized numerators over the running product of uncensored probabilities, exactly the \(W^{C}(t)\) written above. When numerator_model is NULL the numerator is 1 and the weights are unstabilized. Truncation reuses Chapter 9’s pmin(w, quantile(w, p)), here applied across all rows rather than all patients. The fit goes through geepack::geeglm() with id set to the patient identifier, which now does real work: a patient contributes several correlated rows, and treating them as independent observations would understate the standard error on two counts, once for the weights and once for the repeated rows. Clustering on the patient addresses both. The function returns the tidied fit together with the weighted data, so that the weights themselves can be inspected.

res <- ipcw_estimator(
  pp,
  censor_model    = reformulate(c("splines::ns(tstop, 3)", sta_covs), "censored"),
  outcome_formula = reformulate(c("statin", "splines::ns(tstop, 3)"), "event"),
  numerator_model = reformulate("splines::ns(tstop, 3)", "censored")
)

The outcome model is deliberately thin: statin, a spline in time, and nothing else. (A weighted binomial fit also provokes a warning about non-integer successes, since the weights are fractional; it is expected, and the sandwich variance is what we are relying on rather than the likelihood.)

11.4 Weight diagnostics

A weight is a claim about how many unseen patients each observed patient represents, and such a claim should be examined before it is relied upon.

res$data |>
  group_by(interval = ceiling(tstop)) |>
  summarise(rows = n(), mean = mean(w_cens), sd = sd(w_cens),
            max = max(w_cens), .groups = "drop") |>

Two features of the table deserve comment, of which the second is the more important. The stabilized weights average 1.000 with a standard deviation of 0.034, which is the reassuring diagnostic, indicating that the censoring model is not producing a pseudo-population of the wrong size. However, the whole distribution is narrow. The largest weight in the data is 1.27 before truncation, and the weights drift away from 1 only slowly as the intervals accumulate, because a running product of numbers very close to 1 stays close to 1. Kish’s effective sample size, 44,433 of 44,485 rows, records the same fact: these weights cost almost no information.

That narrowness is a finding about this cohort rather than about the method. The weights spread out only to the extent that the covariates in the censoring model predict who drops out, and Chapter 10 already showed that they do so weakly, since every standardized difference between the early-loss patients and everyone else was under 0.10. A censoring model built from variables that do not predict censoring returns probabilities near the overall average, and those cancel almost exactly against the stabilizing numerator.

Truncation, on this evidence, is a formality. Capping at the 99th percentile touches 445 of the 44,485 rows and changes the largest weight from 1.27 to 1.11, so nothing here hangs on it. The habit is worth keeping in any case, since a running product is the kind of weight most liable to misbehave: a single interval with a low estimated probability of remaining uncensored multiplies into every later weight for that patient, and with ten intervals and a richer censoring model, weights in the hundreds are routine. The rule from Chapter 9 carries over, which is to report the untruncated estimate, report it capped, and if they disagree materially, say so rather than choosing between them.

11.5 Did it matter?

The honest test is to fit the same pooled logistic model twice, once with the censoring weights and once with weights of 1, and put the answers beside each other. Since the model is a hazard model, we also multiply the fitted hazards back into risks at the horizons Chapter 10 named.

d      <- res$data
w_one  <- rep(1, nrow(d))
outcome_formula <- reformulate(c("statin", "splines::ns(tstop, 3)"), "event")

wfit <- geepack::geeglm(outcome_formula, id = d$.row_id, weights = d$w_cens,
                        family = binomial(), data = d)
ufit <- geepack::geeglm(outcome_formula, id = d$.row_id, weights = w_one,
                        family = binomial(), data = d)

bind_rows("IPCW weighted" = broom::tidy(wfit, conf.int = TRUE),
          "Unweighted"    = broom::tidy(ufit, conf.int = TRUE),
          .id = "analysis") |>
  filter(term == "statin") |>
  mutate(across(c(estimate, conf.low, conf.high), exp)) |>
  select(analysis, odds_ratio = estimate, conf.low, conf.high) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
analysis odds_ratio conf.low conf.high
IPCW weighted 0.430 0.295 0.628
Unweighted 0.422 0.290 0.616 
grid <- expand_grid(statin = 0:1, tstop = 1:admin_end)

risk_at <- function(fit) {
  grid |>
    mutate(h = predict(fit, newdata = grid, type = "response")) |>
    group_by(statin) |>
    mutate(risk = 1 - cumprod(1 - h)) |>
    ungroup() |>
    filter(tstop %in% c(5, admin_end))
}

bind_rows("IPCW weighted" = risk_at(wfit), "Unweighted" = risk_at(ufit),
          .id = "analysis") |>
  select(analysis, statin, years = tstop, risk) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 4)
analysis statin years risk
IPCW weighted 0 5 0.0560
IPCW weighted 0 10 0.0997
IPCW weighted 1 5 0.0246
IPCW weighted 1 10 0.0444
Unweighted 0 5 0.0564
Unweighted 0 10 0.1014
Unweighted 1 5 0.0243
Unweighted 1 10 0.0443

The weighted odds ratio for statin is 0.430 against 0.422 unweighted, and the five-year risk difference moves from -3.21 percentage points to -3.14. Neither quantity moved materially, and the confidence intervals are effectively identical. The weighted five-year risks, 5.6% without a statin and 2.5% with one, also land near Chapter 10’s Kaplan–Meier estimates of 5.7% and 2.9% — reassurance that the pooled logistic is estimating the same curve, with the remaining gap the price of describing a hazard with three degrees of freedom.

This is the expected result, and it is worth examining rather than apologizing for. Weights near 1 cannot move an estimate, and the weights are near 1 because the variables we measured do not predict who left. The correction was available, we applied it, and the data indicate that the correction was not needed for the censoring that baseline covariates can see. That qualification carries most of the weight of the sentence. If dropping out is driven by something we did not record, such as a symptom that worsened, a decision to stop attending, or the unmeasured prognosis in Chapter 10’s diagram, then the collider is still being conditioned on, the estimate is still biased, and this analysis would look exactly as it does now. A well-behaved weight distribution is evidence that the censoring model found little to correct, and not evidence that there was little to correct. The parallel with unmeasured confounding in Chapter 9 is uncomfortable but exact, and it is the reason for the sensitivity analyses of Chapter 14.

One further element is deliberately missing from the model above. A complete analysis of this question would weight for both problems at once: build Chapter 9’s stabilized treatment weight from a model for statin given sta_covs, build this chapter’s censoring weight for each interval, multiply them per row, and fit the same pooled logistic marginal structural model with the product as its weight. The two weights address different arrows and compose cleanly, and their product is the standard construction for time-to-event marginal structural models. Chapter 12 assembles it, in the service of a harder question than a point treatment.
