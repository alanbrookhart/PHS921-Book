Causal Inference in Epidemiology Using R

Chapter 6 Study design for causal questions

Chapter 3 introduced estimands, Chapter 4 developed the target trial as the protocol against which any observational analysis is implicitly compared, and Chapter 5 showed how a confounding structure can be read off a diagram. Each of those chapters, however, assumed that the rows of the target trial table — who is eligible, what counts as each treatment strategy, when follow-up starts — had already been settled before any adjustment method entered the picture. In this chapter we take up those decisions. We consider three design choices that determine what a causal comparison is capable of estimating, independent of which model eventually gets fit to the data: choosing new users over prevalent users, choosing an active comparator over no treatment at all, and aligning the moment a patient becomes eligible with the moment their follow-up clock starts. If any of the three is specified badly, no amount of standardization, propensity-score weighting, or careful modeling in the chapters ahead will repair it, because the bias has entered through which rows and which person-time were analyzed.

6.1 Design before analysis

An estimand, in the language of Chapter 3, is a contrast between potential outcomes in some population — the ATE if we mean everyone, the ATT if we mean the patients who were actually treated. Which contrast we can identify from a data set is decided well before any regression is fit, by choices that belong entirely to design: who is eligible to enter the cohort, what distinguishes the treatment groups, when each patient’s clock starts, and which group the treated patients are measured against. The target trial protocol of Chapter 4 is essentially a checklist of these decisions, written out in the same seven rows a trial’s own protocol would use, and it is deliberately silent on adjustment: eligibility, treatment strategies, and follow-up can all be specified in full before standardization or weighting is mentioned.

That ordering matters because no statistical method can repair a design that answers the wrong question. If eligibility quietly mixes patients who have been on a drug for years with patients just starting it, the sample no longer corresponds to any one estimand — neither the effect of starting the drug nor the effect of continuing it — no matter how well the eventual model fits. Standardization, propensity scores, and inverse probability weighting, the subjects of Part II, are strategies for closing a measured gap in exchangeability once the design has fixed which patients are being compared and at what moment; they cannot rescue a design that never posed an answerable question. This chapter works through three of the design decisions most responsible for that failure in pharmacoepidemiology, and shows how each is already built into the two cohorts we have used since Chapter 1.

6.2 New-user designs

Chapter 1 mentioned prevalent-user bias in passing. Here we consider why it distorts a comparison and how a new-user design addresses it. A prevalent-user comparison enrolls patients who are already on a drug, of whatever duration, and compares them to patients who are not. Two distinct problems follow from that choice, although they usually occur together. The first is depletion of susceptibles: patients who could not tolerate a drug, or for whom it plainly was not working, tend to stop taking it early and so drop out of the pool of “current users” long before anyone samples that pool. The current users who remain are enriched with patients who tolerated the drug well, which biases any comparison toward the drug appearing safer and more effective than it is for the population that ever started it. The second problem is that covariates measured at the same moment as prevalent exposure may already have been changed by the treatment itself. A blood pressure reading taken after several years on a drug reflects, in part, whatever the drug did to blood pressure; using it to “adjust for confounding” adjusts away part of the treatment effect, or, if some other unmeasured factor drives both that downstream covariate and the outcome, opens the kind of collider path Chapter 5 warned against.

The new-user design avoids both problems by anchoring the cohort to the moment of treatment initiation rather than to some arbitrary later moment of observation. A patient enters the cohort only when we observe them starting the drug, with no recorded use of that drug or its class immediately beforehand, and every covariate used for adjustment is measured at or before that same moment — before treatment has had any chance to change it. This is how ns was built. Among NAMCS office visits from 2005 through 2009, a patient enters ns at the visit at which they are observed initiating an NSAID, with no current NSAID use recorded at that visit, and their initiating prescription — Cox-2 selective or nonselective — fixes which arm, cox2_initiation, they belong to for the rest of the cohort. Of the 2882 patients in ns, 240 are captured this way as Cox-2 initiators and the rest as nonselective initiators; nobody in either group had already been taking an NSAID before the qualifying visit, and nobody’s baseline covariates were measured after the drug had a chance to act on them. ns is therefore already a new-user cohort by construction, although we had not previously named the design principle it follows.

The idea generalizes beyond initiation. Starting a drug is one clinical decision, but stopping one, switching, and intensifying a dose are decisions too, and each can anchor a comparison in the same way: enroll patients at the moment the decision is made, classify them by what was decided, and follow them forward. This framing — the treatment decision design (Brookhart 2015) — is the general form of the new-user design, and it is what allows the target trials of Chapter 4 to be emulated at decision points other than a first prescription; Chapter 12’s treatment strategies rest on this view.

Pharmacoepidemiology has accumulated names for the specific ways these comparisons go wrong, and the names are worth knowing because they are how reviewers will describe your study. Each is a special case of confounding or of misaligned time, and each is addressed by some part of the new-user, active-comparator design.

Healthy user effect. Patients who start and continue a preventive therapy also tend to exercise, undergo screening, and follow other medical advice, so an apparent benefit of the drug may instead reflect the behavior of the patients taking it (Chapter 1). An active comparator mitigates this, since patients in both arms have consulted a physician and accepted a prescription.

Healthy adherer effect. The same phenomenon operating within a trial or a cohort arm: because adherence is itself a marker of health, a comparison of adherent with nonadherent patients confounds the effect of the drug with the disposition of the patients who take it. Design alone does not address this; the protocol weights of Chapter 12 do.

Sick stopper effect. Patients who become ill tend to discontinue treatment, so untreated person-time becomes enriched with patients who are about to experience outcomes. Following new users from a fixed time zero keeps discontinuation downstream of the exposure assignment rather than letting it redefine that assignment.

Protopathic bias. A drug prescribed for the earliest symptoms of an as-yet-undiagnosed disease will appear to cause the disease it was intended to treat. Anchoring time zero at initiation, and where necessary lagging the outcome window, keeps the drug from being blamed for the condition that prompted the prescription.

Depletion of susceptibles. Described above: prevalent users are those who survived the early attrition, so the pool is enriched with patients who tolerate the drug. Enrolling patients at initiation instead samples everyone who ever started.

6.3 Active-comparator designs

A new-user design still leaves open a further choice: new users of the drug of interest, compared to what? The most obvious comparator is patients who did not start any NSAID at all, although for this question that is a poor choice. Whether a physician prescribes an NSAID in the first place is itself a treatment decision, driven by whether a patient has arthritis, joint pain, or another condition that calls for one — and that same decision is entangled with everything connected to needing an NSAID at all, most of which has nothing to do with GI risk specifically. Comparing Cox-2 initiators to non-initiators folds confounding by indication for needing an NSAID together with whatever separately drives the choice between the two drug classes, and the resulting mixture is much harder to reason about than either piece alone.

An active-comparator design removes the first of these by drawing the comparison group from patients who also started an NSAID, of the other class. Every patient in ns, regardless of arm, already needed and received NSAID therapy, so whatever led a physician to prescribe an NSAID in the first place is held approximately fixed across both arms and cannot by itself produce imbalance between them. What can still differ between Cox-2 initiators and nonselective initiators is the narrower set of factors that lead a physician to prefer one NSAID over the other once the decision to prescribe an NSAID has been made: age, history of GI risk, and concurrent steroid use, which is the adjustment set {age, gi_risk, steroids} identified by Chapter 5’s DAG. An active comparator does not eliminate confounding. It narrows the set of variables we have to worry about from everything connected to needing pain treatment down to whatever distinguishes one NSAID from the other, a set that is more plausibly measured in full and more amenable to the adjustment methods of Part II.

We can see how much confounding remains, and along which covariates, by rebuilding Chapter 2’s Table 1 for every covariate in ns_covs rather than the handful used there for illustration. ns_covs mixes two kinds of variables — age and year are numeric, everything else is categorical — so we route each kind through its own helper from R/table1.R, casting year to numeric first since it arrives from NAMCS as a factor.

Even with the comparator restricted to a shared class of drugs, several covariates still separate the two arms by a wide margin. The largest gap is arthritis: 30.0% of nonselective initiators carry an arthritis diagnosis against 46.7% of Cox-2 initiators, a gap of 17 percentage points, larger than any other in either table. Age moves the same way — Cox-2 initiators average 53.4 years against 47.3 among nonselective initiators, a gap of roughly 6 years — and corticosteroid use, though rare overall, is more than four times as common among Cox-2 initiators (2.1% versus 0.5%). Hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis, and PPI use all run a few points higher among Cox-2 initiators as well (22.9% vs 29.6%, 8.1% vs 12.9%, 2.3% vs 5.4%, and 3.0% vs 4.6%).

These imbalances are not arbitrary. Arthritis and the comorbidities that accompany it — hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis, PPI use — are what stands behind the gi_risk node in the diagram, while age and corticosteroid use correspond to the diagram’s age and steroids nodes. Each of these three confounders has an arrow into both cox2 and pud in Chapter 5’s diagram, and each appears here as a substantial imbalance. Sex (58.7% female vs 58.3%) and calendar year (2007.1 vs 2006.8) sit close to balanced, which fits the diagram: neither has an arrow into cox2. Region fits neither pattern: 37.0% of nonselective initiators are from the South against 43.8% of Cox-2 initiators, a gap of 7 points comparable to that for hypertension, although region has no node in Chapter 5’s diagram and no obvious clinical mechanism ties geography to drug choice. An imbalance in Table 1 is not by itself evidence of a confounding path; this one probably reflects regional prescribing habits rather than confounding by GI risk, although it warrants scrutiny rather than being dismissed as either explained or safely ignorable. The gaps that do map onto the diagram — arthritis and the conditions that accompany it, age, and corticosteroid use — are the ones that Part II’s adjustment methods are meant to close.

6.4 Immortal time and other time-zero problems

A design can specify eligibility and the comparator correctly and still fail on a third dimension: when each patient’s follow-up clock starts. Aligned time zero means that three things happen on the same date for every patient: the date they are confirmed eligible, the date they are classified into a treatment strategy, and the date their person-time starts counting toward the outcome. ns satisfies this automatically, because eligibility, drug class, and the start of follow-up are all pinned to the same qualifying visit. The requirement is easy to state, but it is violated whenever a study defines “exposed” using information that could only be known after follow-up had already begun.

A worked hypothetical shows how the violation operates. It is built around a definition of treatment that is common in pharmacoepidemiology: a patient is called a “user” only once a second prescription has been filled, some grace period after the first. Suppose we simulate 20,000 hypothetical patients, each with a true time to death that is, by construction, unrelated to anything about their future drug-taking behavior, and suppose “persistent user” status requires two things: surviving past a 90-day grace period, and an independent adherence habit that is fixed for each patient and has no effect on survival.

set.seed(6)
n <- 20000
grace <- 90
follow_up <- 730

hyp <- tibble(
  id = 1:n,
  death_time = rexp(n, rate = 1 / 2000),
  adherent   = rbinom(n, 1, 0.6)   # a habit unrelated to survival, by construction
) |>
  mutate(
    alive_grace = death_time > grace,
    persistent  = as.integer(alive_grace & adherent == 1),
    died_by_end = as.integer(death_time <= follow_up)
  )

A naive analysis, of the kind a misaligned time zero produces, starts every patient’s clock on day zero and compares mortality risk between “persistent users” and everyone else over the full 730-day window:

naive_tab <- hyp |>
  group_by(persistent) |>
  summarise(n = n(), risk = mean(died_by_end), .groups = "drop")
naive_tab |> knitr::kable(digits = 3)
persistent n risk
0 8538 0.349
1 11462 0.276

Persistent users appear strongly protected, with mortality risk 7.3 percentage points lower than everyone else, even though adherence was given no causal effect on survival in the simulation. The explanation is mechanical rather than clinical. To be labeled “persistent,” a patient had to survive at least 90 days, so the persistent group begins its 730-day follow-up with 90 days during which a death could not have occurred, whatever the composition of that group. This guaranteed event-free interval is immortal time, and it arises entirely because the exposure definition depends on information — survival through the grace period — that was not available at the true time zero, the date treatment actually started.

The remedy is to give every patient the same time zero regardless of group, restricting the comparison to patients who survived to the end of the grace period and starting follow-up at day 90 rather than at day zero.

landmark_tab |> knitr::kable(digits = 3)
adherent n risk
0 7668 0.275
1 11462 0.276

Among the 19130 patients who survived to day 90, adherent and non-adherent patients now have essentially identical mortality risk over the remaining follow-up (0.276 versus 0.275, a difference of 0.001) — indistinguishable from the null effect we built into the simulation. Nothing about any patient differed between the two analyses. Only the point at which follow-up was allowed to begin changed, and that change alone was enough to produce, and then remove, a seven-point “protective” effect.

Chapter 4 flagged this hazard for the sta cohort, where any comparison built around remaining on a statin for some minimum duration is vulnerable to the same mechanism, and left for this chapter the question of which row of the target trial table is violated. It is the Follow-up row, which specifies when person-time starts accruing toward the outcome. Immortal time bias arises whenever the Assignment row — how a patient gets classified into a treatment strategy — is allowed to depend on something observed after the Follow-up row’s start date, rather than at it. A short checklist captures the requirement for any design in this book, ns, sta, or a design of your own:

Component Must be fixed as of the same date as…
Eligibility the date all inclusion criteria are first met
Assignment the date the patient’s treatment strategy is classified
Start of follow-up the date person-time begins accruing toward the outcome

When those three dates coincide, as they do in ns by construction, immortal time cannot arise, because no interval separates the point at which a patient becomes eligible from the start of follow-up in which later information could influence the treatment label. If they are allowed to drift apart, as in the persistent-user hypothetical above, the resulting comparison can produce a spurious association or conceal a real one, and no adjustment method in Part II will correct it. This is much of what specifying a target trial buys: making eligibility, assignment, and the start of follow-up coincide forecloses immortal time bias rather than leaving it to be corrected after the fact, an argument developed by Hernán and colleagues (2016, Journal of Clinical Epidemiology).
