Chapter 4 The randomized trial as the benchmark

Chapter 3 named exchangeability as the condition that made the naive comparison in Chapter 1 fail: Cox-2 initiators and nonselective initiators were not exchangeable, because physicians routed patients toward one drug or the other based on characteristics connected to ulcer risk. That chapter also promised, in passing, that a randomized trial buys exchangeability “by construction,” rather than by an assumption you have to defend with subject-matter argument. In this chapter we make good on that promise, first in code and then in words, by showing why handing the treatment decision to a random number generator rather than to a physician’s judgment produces two groups that are, on average, alike with respect to both measured and unmeasured characteristics. We then consider what a randomized trial can and cannot still promise once patients stop doing what they were assigned to do, using the distinction between intention-to-treat and per-protocol analysis. Finally, we introduce the idea that organizes the rest of this book, the target trial, which is the discipline of writing down the trial one wishes one could run before attempting to emulate it with the data actually available.

4.1 Why randomization works

Confounding by indication, as Chapter 1 described it, arises because treatment assignment is a function of patient characteristics connected to the outcome: a physician who suspects a patient is at elevated gastrointestinal risk is more likely to reach for the Cox-2 selective drug for exactly that patient, so Cox-2 initiators end up looking sicker on the very dimension the drug is meant to protect. Randomization addresses this at its source by making assignment a function of nothing about the patient at all, neither age nor arthritis history nor anything else a physician can observe, and, equally important, nothing a physician cannot observe. It is this last property that makes randomization more powerful than any adjustment strategy in this book. Matching, standardization, and propensity scores can balance only those covariates someone thought to measure, whereas a coin flip balances everything, including confounders no one ever recorded, because the assignment mechanism does not consult any characteristic of the patient, known or unknown. Formally, when \(A\) is generated by a physical randomization device rather than by clinical judgment, \(A\) is statistically independent of baseline covariates \(L\) and of both potential outcomes \(Y(1), Y(0)\) by the mechanics of assignment, so that the exchangeability condition from Chapter 3 is delivered as a feature of the design rather than assumed.

We do not have an actual randomized trial of Cox-2 selectivity, only the ns cohort, in which treatment was a physician’s choice. We can, however, ask a narrower question of the data we do have: what would the Table 1 comparison and the crude risk comparison look like if the label “Cox-2 initiator” had been handed out by a coin flip instead of by a physician? mutate(a_rand = sample(cox2_init)) answers that question by shuffling which of the 2882 rows get called “1,” so that the same 240 Cox-2 labels in the data land on that many patients chosen completely at random, while every actual covariate and every actual outcome stays exactly as recorded. This is a relabeling exercise, not a new trial: nobody’s peptic ulcer disease status changes, because pud is not regenerated. The only thing the random draw decides is which of the real patients we call “arm 1” versus “arm 0” for the purpose of the comparison that follows.

Look at arthritis first: under the real, physician-chosen assignment the gap between groups is 17 percentage points (46.7% among Cox-2 initiators versus 30.0% among nonselective initiators), which is large, and is the kind of systematic imbalance Chapter 2 already flagged when it introduced this Table 1. Under the randomly permuted label, the same comparison shrinks to 4.8 points. Diabetes behaves similarly: a real gap of 4.8 points falls to 0.2 under random assignment. Hypertension is an instructive exception, in that its gap under the random label happens to come out to 6.7 points, identical to the real gap of 6.7. Rather than indicating that hypertension remains confounded after randomization, this coincidence is a reminder that any single random split is only one draw among many possible draws, and that a single draw will not, by chance alone, look perfectly balanced on every variable. What randomization guarantees is that mismatches of this kind are unsystematic: if we rerun the shuffle with a different seed, the hypertension coincidence disappears, while arthritis and diabetes continue to come back close to balanced, since approximate balance is what randomization delivers on average rather than in any particular draw.

The same logic applies to the outcome itself. Because pud was never regenerated, the treated and untreated groups defined by a_rand are being compared on outcomes that already happened, for reasons that had nothing to do with a_rand.

contrasts <- bind_rows(
  ns |> summarise(
    assignment = "real (cox2_init)",
    risk_0 = mean(pud[cox2_init == 0]),
    risk_1 = mean(pud[cox2_init == 1])
  ),
  ns_rand |> summarise(
    assignment = "randomly permuted (a_rand)",
    risk_0 = mean(pud[a_rand == 0]),
    risk_1 = mean(pud[a_rand == 1])
  )
) |> mutate(diff = risk_1 - risk_0)
contrasts |> knitr::kable(digits = 4)
assignment risk_0 risk_1 diff
real (cox2_init) 0.0977 0.125 0.0273
randomly permuted (a_rand) 0.0999 0.100 0.0001

The real crude contrast reproduces Chapter 1’s finding: Cox-2 initiators have a 2.7-point higher crude risk of PUD than nonselective initiators, the wrong-signed, confounded comparison this whole book exists to fix. The randomly permuted contrast, computed from the identical set of recorded pud values, comes out to 0.01 points, which is indistinguishable from zero. No patient’s actual outcome changed between the two rows of that table; the only difference is which label we used to split the data into two groups. When the label carries no information about who received it, as a coin flip guarantees, the crude contrast is an unbiased estimate of a null effect, and it recovers one. When the label is instead a physician’s judgment, correlated with everything that judgment was trying to account for, the same arithmetic turns up a gap that has nothing to do with the drug. This is why the randomized trial is described as the benchmark. The reason is not that any one trial is guaranteed a perfectly matched Table 1, since chance imbalance of the sort seen for hypertension above does occur, but rather that whatever mismatch remains is due to chance alone, which is the kind of uncertainty that ordinary confidence intervals are built to quantify. Confounding by indication is a different kind of error, and no amount of additional data makes it smaller, because it is not noise. Every design and every adjustment method in the rest of this book is judged by how close it comes to the null contrast that randomization produced in this section without any adjustment at all.

4.2 Intention-to-treat and per-protocol

A coin flip decides the arm a patient is assigned to; it does not decide what that patient actually does afterward. In a real Cox-2 trial, some patients randomized to the Cox-2 arm would stop the drug early because of side effects or cost, some randomized to nonselective NSAIDs would switch to Cox-2 therapy if their joint pain persisted, and some in either arm would take over-the-counter NSAIDs the protocol never intended to capture. Once assignment and actual treatment can diverge, a trial supports (at least) two different analyses, and they answer two different questions.

The intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis compares patients according to the arm they were randomized to, regardless of what treatment they actually went on to receive. Writing \(A\) for randomized assignment, the ITT contrast is \(E[Y \mid A = 1] - E[Y \mid A = 0]\), computed on the groups the randomization device itself created. Because \(A\) is here the coin flip, ITT preserves exchangeability as constructed, and the comparison remains a contrast between groups that differ, on average, only by that coin flip, however much nonadherence occurs afterward. For this reason ITT is the default primary analysis in nearly every large randomized trial, being the one contrast that inherits randomization’s guarantee without further qualification.

That guarantee, however, comes at a cost of interpretation, because ITT does not estimate the effect of taking the assigned drug. It estimates the effect of being assigned to a strategy, which is a blend of the effect among patients who followed it and the effect among patients who did not. When nonadherence is common, ITT effects are typically attenuated toward the null relative to the effect of full adherence, so that a drug that would be beneficial if everyone took it as directed can produce a modest or null ITT result if enough assigned patients discontinue treatment. The per-protocol analysis attempts to recover the more specific question by restricting to, or reclassifying patients according to, what they actually did, comparing adherers to the Cox-2 strategy against adherers to the nonselective strategy rather than comparing the arms as randomized. Although this is often the clinically more interesting question, once patients are grouped by what they actually did rather than by what the coin flip assigned them, the randomization at baseline no longer protects the comparison. Whatever led a given patient to adhere rather than switch or discontinue (eg, symptom severity, general health, health literacy, or tolerance of side effects) may also be connected to the outcome, which is the mechanism Chapter 1 called the healthy-user effect, now operating inside a randomized trial rather than across an observational cohort. A naive per-protocol comparison can therefore be confounded even though the trial that produced the data was, at the point of randomization, perfectly randomized.

The sta cohort makes these concerns concrete without requiring a real trial. Because sta is observational, and nobody was randomized to start a statin, any comparison of patients who initiated and stayed on a statin for some minimum duration against everyone else is already per-protocol in spirit, and already vulnerable to the same self-selection just described. It is vulnerable to a further problem as well: misclassifying the follow-up time before a patient meets the “adherer” criterion can build survival into the definition of one of the groups, a distortion called immortal time bias that Chapter 6 introduces on its own terms. Chapter 12 develops the clone-censor-weight approach specifically to answer a per-protocol-style question — what would happen if patients followed a specific treatment strategy for its full duration — using observational data like sta, without letting either kind of bias back in.

4.3 The target trial

Every method in this book adjusts observational data so that it can stand in for a randomized trial. Hernán and Robins (2016) turned that observation into a discipline, called the target trial framework: for any causal question one wants to answer with observational data, one first specifies, in full protocol detail, the randomized trial that would answer it, as though funding, ethics, and recruitment were not obstacles, and only then describes how each piece of that protocol will be emulated using the data actually available. Almost none of the trials specified this way will ever be run. The value of writing the protocol anyway is that it brings every design decision a trial would have to make (who is eligible, what counts as each treatment strategy, when follow-up starts, and how the outcome is defined) into the open before any adjustment method is chosen, instead of leaving those decisions implicit in a modeling script.

Here is that protocol, written out for the Cox-2 question this book has been asking since Chapter 1:

Protocol component Target trial Emulation using ns
Eligibility Adults beginning NSAID therapy, with no NSAID use in the preceding period Same criterion, applied when ns was assembled: new users only
Treatment strategies (1) Initiate a Cox-2 selective NSAID; (2) initiate a nonselective NSAID cox2_init == 1 versus cox2_init == 0 at the qualifying prescription
Assignment Random allocation to strategy (1) or (2) Not randomized; approximated by conditioning on measured confounders (ns_covs) under the conditional exchangeability assumption from Chapter 3
Follow-up Begins at randomization (“time zero”) and continues to the outcome, loss to follow-up, or a fixed administrative end Begins at the qualifying prescription date, ends at incident PUD, loss to follow-up, or the administrative end
Outcome Incident peptic ulcer disease pud == 1 observed during follow-up
Causal contrast Intention-to-treat comparison of the two assigned strategies ITT analog: compare all patients who initiated each strategy at time zero, regardless of later switching or discontinuation
Analysis plan Risk difference or risk ratio between arms, estimated directly from randomized groups Same contrast, estimated with an adjustment method (standardization, propensity scores, or IPTW — Chapters 7 through 9) that stands in for randomization

Six of these seven rows can already be written down for ns with no controversy, in the same language a trial protocol would use: the eligibility criteria are those used to identify new users, the two strategies are already coded in cox2_init, follow-up already has a defined start and end, the outcome is already defined as pud, and even the causal contrast and analysis plan can be stated without needing to resolve anything about confounding first. Only the Assignment row cannot be filled in as “randomized,” because assignment was not randomized, and that single row is where the rest of this book does its work. Standardization and outcome regression, propensity scores and inverse probability weighting, and instrumental variables and sensitivity analysis are each a different technical strategy for filling in that one row with something that behaves enough like a coin flip to make the causal contrast in the last row credible. Chapters 6, 10, and 12 return to a version of this same protocol, adapted for study design choices, for survival outcomes, and for the dynamic treatment strategies studied in sta, although the structure of the argument is the same in each case: specify the trial first, then argue, row by row, for how well the observational data emulate it.
