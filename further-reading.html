Causal Inference in Epidemiology Using R

E Further reading

This book is deliberately narrow, with two running examples, one estimator per chapter, and just enough theory to justify each one. The sources below go further, in a number of directions.

On the framework as a whole. Miguel Hernán and James Robins, Causal Inference: What If (Chapman & Hall/CRC) covers the same potential-outcomes framework at greater depth and rigor, with a fuller treatment of time-varying treatments, g-methods, and the assumptions each identification strategy needs. It is available free online and is the natural next stop after this course.

On where the framework came from. Two short papers are well worth a careful read.

  • Holland (1986, JASA) — statistics and causal inference: the paper that set the two side by side and fixed much of the potential-outcomes vocabulary Chapter 3 uses.
  • Hernán (2004, JECH) — a definition of a causal effect, written for epidemiologists, insisting that we say what an effect is a statement about before choosing anything to estimate it with.

On causal diagrams. Chapter 5 uses DAGs as a working tool; these two papers are where the tool and its most useful application were set out for epidemiology.

  • Greenland, Pearl, and Robins (1999, Epidemiology) — causal diagrams for epidemiologic research: the paper that introduced DAGs to the field, with the backdoor reasoning Chapter 5 runs on.
  • Hernán, Hernández-Díaz, and Robins (2004, Epidemiology) — a structural approach to selection bias, which recasts a long list of named biases as conditioning on a collider. The source for Chapter 5’s selection bias section, and the reason Chapters 10 and 11 can treat censoring and survivorship as the same problem.

On epidemiologic method more broadly. Kenneth Rothman, Sander Greenland, and Timothy Lash, Modern Epidemiology (Lippincott Williams & Wilkins), is the standard reference for study design, bias, and measurement issues that sit upstream of any causal estimator — how a cohort or case-control study is assembled, what confounding and selection bias look like in practice, and how the field has thought about validity before “causal inference” was its own subfield.

On the target trial. The framework Chapter 4 adopts and Chapters 6 and 12 keep returning to.

  • Hernán and Robins (2016, AJE) — using big data to emulate a target trial when a randomized trial is not available: the paper that named the framework and the source of Chapter 4’s protocol table.
  • Hernán et al. (2016, Journal of Clinical Epidemiology) — specifying a target trial prevents immortal time bias, which is the argument behind Chapter 6’s insistence that eligibility, assignment, and start of follow-up share a date.

On design in healthcare databases. Both papers bear directly on Chapter 6.

  • Ray (2003, AJE) — the case for new-user designs, and what a cohort of prevalent users quietly does to a comparison.
  • Brookhart (2015, AJE) — the treatment decision design: the general form of the new-user idea, anchoring comparisons at any clinical decision point, and useful background for target trial emulation.
  • Schneeweiss et al. (2019, Annals of Internal Medicine) — a graphical convention for depicting longitudinal study designs, which makes an argument about eligibility and time zero reviewable at a glance.

On standardization and the g-formula. The machinery of Chapter 7, and why a regression coefficient is not the estimand.

  • Robins (1986, Mathematical Modelling) — the paper that introduced the g-formula, of which Chapter 7’s predict-then-average recipe is the simplest case.
  • Greenland, Robins, and Pearl (1999, Statistical Science) — confounding and collapsibility in causal inference: the reference for why a conditional and a marginal odds ratio differ even with no confounding at all.

On confounding control and propensity scores. These take up the questions Chapter 8 raises and then sets aside.

  • Rubin (2008, Annals of Applied Statistics) — for objective causal inference, design trumps analysis: the argument behind Chapter 8’s claim that building the score without looking at the outcome is most of what the score is for.
  • Brookhart et al. (2006, AJE) — variable selection for propensity score models: which covariates belong in a treatment model, and what including or excluding one does to bias and variance.
  • Brookhart et al. (2010, Medical Care) — a survey of the options for confounding control in healthcare database studies, of which this book works through a few in detail.
  • Schneeweiss et al. (2009, Epidemiology) — high-dimensional propensity score adjustment: the automated proxy-selection algorithm Chapter 8 mentions but does not implement.
  • Lipsitch, Tchetgen Tchetgen, and Cohen (2010, Epidemiology) — negative controls, and what a non-null association with an outcome the treatment cannot cause does and does not establish. The source for Chapter 14’s section on them.

On weighting and marginal structural models. Chapter 9’s estimator, in the papers that established it.

  • Robins, Hernán, and Brumback (2000, Epidemiology) — marginal structural models and causal inference in epidemiology: the source of the model class and of the name Chapter 9 explains.
  • Cole and Hernán (2008, AJE) — constructing inverse probability weights for marginal structural models, which is the practical companion to Chapter 9’s stabilization and truncation.

On what to report. All three argue for a particular quantity rather than a particular estimator.

  • Cole et al. (2015, AJE) — risk: a case for reporting risks and risk contrasts rather than ratios of hazards, which is why Chapters 10 through 12 keep returning to cumulative incidence.
  • Hernán (2010, Epidemiology) — the hazards of hazard ratios: the short editorial behind Chapter 10’s built-in selection argument, and the reason this book treats a hazard ratio as a modeling device rather than an estimand.
  • Hernán and Robins (2017, NEJM) — per-protocol analyses of pragmatic trials, the argument behind Chapter 4’s per-protocol discussion and Chapter 12’s censoring and weighting.

On instrumental variables. Chapter 13 leans on all three.

  • Angrist, Imbens, and Rubin (1996, JASA) — identification of causal effects using instrumental variables, and the paper that made the complier-average interpretation standard.
  • Hernán and Robins (2006, Epidemiology) — instruments for causal inference: an epidemiologist’s dream? The source of Chapter 13’s statement of the three conditions, and of its skepticism about them.
  • Brookhart et al. (2006, Epidemiology) — preference-based instrumental variables: what makes a prescriber’s preference a defensible instrument, and where that assumption tends to break.

On sensitivity analysis. The two devices Chapter 14 builds, each in the paper that introduced it.

  • Schneeweiss (2006, Pharmacoepidemiology and Drug Safety) — sensitivity analysis and external adjustment for unmeasured confounders, the source of Chapter 14’s term and of the bias-factor parameterization it uses.
  • VanderWeele and Ding (2017, Annals of Internal Medicine) — sensitivity analysis in observational research: introducing the E-value, and the derivation Chapter 14’s second device rests on.

On the software. Yihui Xie, J.J. Allaire, and Garrett Grolemund, R Markdown: The Definitive Guide and the bookdown documentation at bookdown.org, cover the publishing system this book itself is written in. Hadley Wickham, Mine Çetinkaya-Rundel, and Garrett Grolemund, R for Data Science (“R4DS”, also free online), is the best general reference for the tidyverse functions — dplyr verbs, ggplot2, purrr — that Chapter 2 introduces only as much of as this book needs.
