D Generalized linear models for causal contrasts

Chapters 7 through 9 fit a regression and then read either a single coefficient or a pair of predicted means as a causal contrast. Which contrast the coefficient carries is settled by the link function of the model, and those chapters chose links without stopping to explain the family of models they come from. This appendix is that explanation.

A generalized linear model writes the conditional mean of the outcome as a transformation of a linear predictor,

\[g\bigl(E[Y \mid X]\bigr) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p,\]

where \(g\) is the link function and the family names the assumed relationship between the mean and the variance. McCullagh and Nelder assembled the class in this form. What matters for us is that the same fitting machinery returns a different causal contrast for each link, with the data and the covariates held fixed.

Three combinations do nearly all of the work in this book. The identity link with the gaussian family regresses \(Y\) on treatment directly, so its coefficient is a difference of means, which for a binary outcome is a risk difference. The log link with the Poisson family gives a coefficient that exponentiates to a ratio of means, a risk ratio when \(Y\) is binary; the likelihood is then deliberately wrong, since a 0/1 outcome is not a count, although the mean model is correct and the coefficient still estimates a log risk ratio. That is the modified Poisson device Chapter 9 uses. The logit link with the binomial family is ordinary logistic regression, and its coefficient exponentiates to an odds ratio. All three fit with the same call to glm(), changed only in family =.

links <- list(
  "identity / gaussian" = gaussian(link = "identity"),
  "log / Poisson"       = poisson(link = "log"),
  "logit / binomial"    = binomial(link = "logit")
)

glm_tab <- imap_dfr(links, function(fam, label) {
  fit <- glm(pud ~ cox2_init, family = fam, data = ns)
  b   <- broom::tidy(fit) |> filter(term == "cox2_init")
  tibble(
    link        = label,
    contrast    = c("risk difference", "risk ratio", "odds ratio")[
                    match(label, names(links))],
    coefficient = b$estimate,
    value       = if (fam$link == "identity") b$estimate else exp(b$estimate)
  )
})

glm_tab |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3,
               caption = "Three links, three crude contrasts, one cohort.")
Table D.1: Three links, three crude contrasts, one cohort.
link contrast coefficient value
identity / gaussian risk difference 0.027 0.027
log / Poisson risk ratio 0.247 1.280
logit / binomial odds ratio 0.278 1.320

Reading down the value column, Cox-2 initiation is associated with a crude risk difference of +2.7 percentage points, a crude risk ratio of 1.28, and a crude odds ratio of 1.32. These are crude associations: nothing has been adjusted, the two arms differ at baseline in the ways Chapter 2 tabulated, and the outcome in ns is simulated. Adjustment is the business of the body chapters, and each of them reaches one of these three contrasts by a route that gives it a causal reading. The odds ratio sits further from the null than the risk ratio, which is the arithmetic of an uncommon outcome rather than a finding, and the non-collapsibility Chapter 7 discusses is a further reason to prefer the first two rows when the question is about risk.

Two of the three fits report a standard error we do not want. For the gaussian fit on a binary outcome the variance depends on the mean rather than being constant, and for the Poisson fit the assumed variance exceeds the Bernoulli variance, so in both cases the model-based standard error comes from a variance model we know to be false. A robust, or sandwich, standard error estimates the variance of the estimating equation from the data instead, which repairs the interval and leaves the point estimate alone. Chapters 9 and 11 obtain it from geepack::geeglm(), which also takes the weights those chapters need.

imap_dfr(links[1:2], function(fam, label) {
  m <- glm(pud ~ cox2_init, family = fam, data = ns)
  g <- geepack::geeglm(pud ~ cox2_init, id = seq_len(nrow(ns)),
                       family = fam, data = ns)
  tibble(link = label,
         se_model  = broom::tidy(m)$std.error[2],
         se_robust = broom::tidy(g)$std.error[2])
}) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 4,
               caption = "Model-based and robust standard errors for the same coefficient.")
Table D.2: Model-based and robust standard errors for the same coefficient.
link se_model se_robust
identity / gaussian 0.0202 0.0221
log / Poisson 0.1929 0.1807

The two disagreements run in opposite directions, the robust error being the larger one for the identity link and the smaller one for the log link. Neither direction is guaranteed in general, which is the reason to compute the robust error rather than to reason about which way a misspecified variance will err.

In the course, session 8 takes up generalized linear models before the propensity-score sessions that follow, and every body chapter from Chapter 7 onward assumes that material. This appendix is where a reader who wants it written down once can find it.
