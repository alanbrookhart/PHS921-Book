Causal Inference in Epidemiology Using R

Chapter 1 Why causal inference?

1.1 Three kinds of questions

Epidemiologic analyses of healthcare data — electronic health records, administrative claims, disease registries — can serve several distinct scientific purposes, and it is worth separating them at the outset, because the methods in this book apply to only one of them.

The first purpose is description. Descriptive questions ask what is happening in a population: How many patients in North Carolina Medicaid have asthma? Which counties have the highest rates? How many patients will develop asthma this year? Answering such questions well requires careful attention to how conditions are defined and to who is captured in the data, but it does not require comparing groups of patients or reasoning about what would happen under a different course of action.

The second purpose is prediction. Predictive questions include classification problems — does this radiological image show a patient with lung damage from asthma? — and risk prediction problems — which patients are at highest risk of being admitted for an asthma exacerbation? A model built for prediction is judged by how accurately it predicts, and its inputs need no causal interpretation: a variable can be an excellent predictor of an outcome without causing it. This is why the common phrase “risk factors for admission” is ambiguous — it may describe variables that merely flag high-risk patients, or variables that, if changed, would change the risk.

The third purpose is causal. What is the effect of treatment with a biologic, versus standard of care, on the one-year risk of admission for an asthma exacerbation among newly diagnosed patients? Which patient subgroups are most likely to benefit? What is the effect of reducing copayments on adherence to guideline-recommended asthma treatments? What is the effect of a spike in fine particulate air pollution on emergency department visits for asthma? Each of these asks what would happen to a population if something — a treatment, a policy, an exposure — were different from what it was.

This book is about the third kind of question. It builds a narrative around variations on two of them: 1) Does starting a Cox-2 selective NSAID result in a lower risk of peptic ulcer disease than starting a nonselective NSAID? and 2) Does starting a statin reduce the risk of cardiovascular events compared with no treatment in a broad routine-care population? Both ask what would happen to a group of patients if a particular treatment decision changed, and both are the kind of question epidemiologists are asked to answer using data collected for other purposes.

1.2 Why not just run a trial?

The natural first response to a causal question about a treatment is to propose a randomized trial. Randomization ensures that the groups being compared are, on average, alike at baseline, so a difference in their outcomes can be attributed to the treatment; Chapter 4 develops this argument carefully, because the trial is the benchmark against which every analysis in this book is implicitly judged. If a trial could be run for every causal question in medicine, there would be little need for the rest of this book. Several forces ensure that it cannot.

First, trials are expensive and slow. The ALLHAT trial randomized 33,357 patients with hypertension and at least one cardiovascular risk factor to one of three classes of antihypertensive drugs and followed them for between three and eight years. The trial established that thiazide diuretics were as good as or superior to the newer, more expensive alternatives for every outcome studied — a finding that still shapes hypertension guidelines — but it took eight years to complete and cost roughly $120 million. Only a small fraction of the clinically important comparisons in medicine will ever command resources on that scale.

Second, it is often unclear who would pay. Head-to-head comparative trials are commercially risky for manufacturers: in the PROVE-IT trial, the sponsor’s statin proved inferior to a competitor’s more intensive regimen, a result that reshaped practice at the sponsor’s expense. A manufacturer weighing whether to fund a comparison against a rival product must accept the possibility of financing the demonstration that its own drug is worse.

Third, the efficacy a trial estimates may differ from the effectiveness a treatment achieves in routine care. Denosumab, an injectable biologic for osteoporosis, reduced fracture risk in its pivotal placebo-controlled trial; whether it prevents fractures in real-world use depends on matters the trial protocol controlled — whether patients return for their injections on schedule, and how the medication is offered and covered by insurers. Nonexperimental studies can provide evidence about real-world effectiveness that a trial, by design, cannot. The second of this book’s two questions — whether starting a statin reduces cardiovascular risk in a broad routine-care population — is a question of exactly this kind.

Fourth, trials often exclude the patients in whom a treatment will actually be used: the elderly, patients with chronic comorbidities such as end-stage renal disease or liver disease, racial and ethnic minorities, patients with limited access to the healthcare system, pregnant women, and children. For these groups, evidence about a treatment’s effects must frequently come from data generated by routine care.

Finally, a trial may be unethical or impractical. Antipsychotic medications, approved to treat schizophrenia, came to be widely used off-label to manage behavioral disturbances in elderly patients with dementia. Placebo-controlled trials of the newer atypical agents were conducted in this population, and a meta-analysis of those trials by the FDA found an increased risk of death among patients randomized to the atypical drugs. The finding created a clinical dilemma: should physicians switch patients to the older, conventional antipsychotics? Those agents have many known side effects and a poor safety profile of their own, and a head-to-head trial in frail elderly patients — with death as the anticipated outcome — will never be done. The question could only be answered by analyzing existing data. We will return to how it was answered at the end of this chapter.

When a trial cannot be run, the causal question does not go away; it falls to nonexperimental studies, and with them comes the central problem this book is organized around.

1.3 The central problem: confounding

A nonexperimental study must answer its question with data generated by routine care — electronic health records, administrative claims, disease registries. Because such data were not collected as part of a designed study, many variables the researcher might wish to have remain unrecorded, and the meaning of the variables that are available is often unclear.

One of the principal problems of such studies is confounding — a systematic difference between the group of patients exposed to an intervention and a chosen comparator group. Comparing patients who took a treatment with patients who did not yields an estimate of an association between treatment and outcome. It answers a question of the form “How did the risk of peptic ulcer disease differ between patients who initiated a Cox-2 selective versus a nonselective NSAID?” — a question about a difference in outcomes between two groups of patients as they actually existed, not a causal question. Confounding arises when the factors that influence physician treatment decisions and patient medication use are also independent determinants of health outcomes.

Those factors are rarely simple. The use of a medical intervention is determined by system-, physician-, and patient-level variables that often interact in complex and poorly understood ways. A physician’s treatment decision may be based on an evaluation of the patient’s health status and prognosis, the physician’s past experience with the medication, or an assessment of the patient’s ability and willingness to take the medication as prescribed. Patients may initiate and remain adherent to a new therapy because of their health beliefs, their preferences about treatment, their trust in the physician, the out-of-pocket cost of the medication, or their perceptions of their disease risk and the benefits of treatment. Initiation and adherence may also depend on a patient’s physical and cognitive abilities. Each of these variables may directly affect health outcomes or be related to them through indirect pathways. From this process, several recognizable patterns of bias result; they recur often enough in pharmacoepidemiology to have earned names.

A common and often intractable form is confounding by indication, sometimes called channeling. It arises from good medical practice: physicians tend to prescribe medications to, and perform procedures on, the patients who are most likely to benefit. Because it is often difficult to measure medical indications and underlying disease severity and prognosis, confounding by indication often makes medications appear to cause the outcomes they are meant to prevent. Statins, lipid-lowering drugs that reduce the risk of cardiovascular events, tend to be prescribed to patients perceived to be at increased cardiovascular risk, and incomplete control of those risk factors can make statins appear to cause rather than prevent cardiovascular events. The same logic runs through the first of our two questions: Cox-2 selective NSAIDs were developed, and are often chosen, specifically to reduce gastrointestinal risk in patients thought to need that protection, so Cox-2 initiators can appear to fare worse with respect to peptic ulcer disease not because the drug fails to protect them but because they were a higher-risk group at baseline. If the magnitude of that confounding is large enough, the apparent effect of a genuinely protective drug can be reversed.

A second pattern is the healthy user effect. Patients who initiate a preventive medication may be more likely than other patients to engage in other healthy, prevention-oriented behaviors: to seek out preventive healthcare services, exercise regularly, moderate their alcohol consumption, and avoid unsafe and unhealthy activities. Incomplete adjustment for such behaviors can make use of a preventive medication spuriously associated with reduced risk of a wide range of adverse health outcomes. Patients who adhere to a treatment once started may likewise be more likely to engage in other healthy behaviors — the closely related healthy adherer effect. Strong evidence for it comes from a meta-analysis of randomized trials in which adherence to placebo was associated with reduced mortality. This is clearly not an effect of the placebo; it reflects the characteristics of patients who take a medication as prescribed.

Related phenomena operate at the other end of the health spectrum. Patients who are functionally impaired — who have difficulty performing daily activities of living — may be less able to visit a physician or pharmacy, and therefore less likely to fill prescriptions and receive preventive healthcare services, a difference that can exaggerate the apparent benefit of prescription medications, vaccines, and screening tests. Similarly, patients who are perceived by a physician to be close to death may be less likely to be prescribed preventive medications, and patients may themselves discontinue preventive medications when their health deteriorates. These mechanisms arise through different processes but have the same effect: decreased exposure to preventive medications among the patients at highest risk of death, which can produce a substantially decreased mortality risk among users of statins and other preventive drugs compared with apparently similar nonusers.

A final pattern worth naming here, because it shapes how this book’s cohorts are constructed, is prevalent-user bias: comparing people already on a treatment to people never treated mixes in everyone who started the drug, tolerated it, and stayed on it long enough to be captured in the data, while excluding the patients who stopped early because of side effects or lack of benefit. The comparisons in this book are built around new users — patients captured at the moment they start a drug, as ns and sta are — specifically to avoid folding that kind of survivorship into the treatment groups before the analysis begins.

These concerns are not hypothetical. Observational studies of postmenopausal hormone therapy in the 1990s reported that use was associated with substantially lower risk of coronary heart disease; when the Women’s Health Initiative randomized women to hormone therapy or placebo, the trial found an increased risk. The discrepancy did not arise from careless analysis. Rather, women who used hormone therapy were healthier, more health-conscious, and more closely monitored than women who did not — the healthy user effect operating at scale — and that difference was not fully removed by adjustment for the variables the studies had measured.

If a body of careful nonexperimental research can get the sign of an effect wrong, then a large or richly detailed data set is not by itself sufficient. Indeed, the property of being a confounder is a question of causality, not of statistics: it is not identifiable, or testable, in the data themselves, so no variable selection procedure or statistical model can by itself guarantee an unbiased estimate, even in a very large study. What is needed is a framework, grounded in subject-matter knowledge, that says when an association can be given a causal interpretation and what to do when it cannot. The rest of the book describes such a framework and how the analysis can be implemented in R.

1.4 Causal inference, old and new

Learning about causes from nonexperimental data is not a new ambition in epidemiology. In 1854, John Snow traced a cholera outbreak in London to a single water pump on Broad Street; the company supplying the pump drew its water downstream from a sewage outflow, and the outbreak subsided after the pump handle was removed. The investigation provided early support for the germ theory of disease — that cholera was caused by something in the water — against the then-prevailing miasma theory, which attributed it to bad air.

What is new is the formality. Modern causal inference restates questions like Snow’s as questions about well-defined interventions: What would have happened had the pump handle not been removed? How many cases would have been prevented had it been removed a day earlier? These are counterfactual quantities — statements about outcomes under conditions that did not occur — and Chapter 3 introduces the notation and assumptions that make them precise. As a formal discipline, causal inference is young: it took shape over roughly the past three decades, developing in parallel in statistics, epidemiology, economics, and computer science. Its methods are now widely used and increasingly accepted by drug regulatory agencies, studies built on them appear regularly in leading medical journals, and the 2021 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences was awarded for work on drawing causal conclusions from observational data.

The antipsychotic dilemma described earlier in this chapter was, in fact, answered this way. Using Medicare claims data, investigators compared elderly patients who newly started a conventional antipsychotic with those who newly started an atypical agent — roughly 23,000 new users in all — using both conventional adjustment methods and an instrumental variable analysis that exploited variation in physicians’ prescribing preferences, a design developed in Chapter 13. The primary finding was an approximately 37% higher risk of death within 180 days among new users of the conventional agents. No single nonexperimental study settles such a question, but the result argued against switching patients to the older drugs, and no trial has emerged to contradict it.

What made that study credible is worth noticing, because it previews much of this book. The comparison was between two active treatments with similar indications, so the patients being compared were far more alike at baseline than treated patients and untreated patients would have been, limiting confounding by indication. The outcome window was short, leaving less room for changes in treatment and health status to accumulate over follow-up. Both the exposure and the outcome could be measured well in the available claims data. And the cohorts were restricted to new users, avoiding the prevalent-user bias described above. This combination — the new-user, active-comparator cohort with a well-measured, short-term outcome — is something of a sweet spot for pharmacoepidemiology, and it is the basis of most pharmacoepidemiologic investigations, including the ns cohort at the center of this book. The later parts of the book extend beyond that sweet spot, to questions involving sustained treatment strategies, censoring, and unmeasured confounding, where more is asked of both the data and the methods.

1.5 A first look at the data

We will spend the whole book with the two cohorts introduced properly in the next chapter, although it is worth looking at the simplest possible comparison in one of them now, before any adjustment, to see what an unadjusted answer to a causal question looks like. In ns, the cohort of new NSAID users, patients either initiated a nonselective NSAID or a Cox-2 selective inhibitor, and the outcome of interest is incident peptic ulcer disease (PUD). The crude risk of PUD in each group follows from a single group_by() and summarise():

risk_tab <- ns |>
  group_by(cox2_initiation) |>
  summarise(n = n(), risk = mean(pud))

risk_tab |>
  knitr::kable(
    col.names = c("Initiated drug", "N", "Risk of incident PUD"),
    digits = c(0, 0, 3)
  )
Initiated drug N Risk of incident PUD
No 2642 0.098
Yes 240 0.125

Among the 2642 patients who initiated a nonselective NSAID, 9.8% went on to develop incident PUD; among the 240 patients who initiated a Cox-2 selective drug, the figure was 12.5%.

Is that comparison an estimate of the causal effect of Cox-2 selectivity on peptic ulcer risk? It is not, and there is reason to think it is far from one. Nothing in the code above accounts for why a given patient started one drug rather than the other. This is exactly the setting for the confounding by indication described above: a patient with a history of arthritis, prior GI bleeding, or concurrent corticosteroid or aspirin use is more likely to be steered toward the Cox-2 selective option. If Cox-2 initiators are systematically older or sicker in ways connected to ulcer risk, as we will confirm with a proper Table 1 in the next chapter, then the two rows of this table are not comparing like with like, and the crude risk difference above combines whatever causal effect the drug has with the baseline differences between the groups. Separating those two components, that is, asking what the comparison would look like if the two groups had been alike at baseline, is the problem the rest of the book addresses.

1.6 What this book covers

The book is organized in four parts. Part I, Foundations, which this chapter opens, builds the conceptual vocabulary before any adjustment method is introduced: potential outcomes, the randomized trial as the benchmark against which a causal analysis is implicitly compared, causal diagrams for reasoning about which variables require adjustment, and the elements of study design that turn a causal question into an analyzable data set. Part II, Core Methods, covers the two families of tools most observational analyses reach for first: outcome regression and standardization, and propensity scores, culminating in inverse probability of treatment weighting. Part III, Time, Censoring, and Survival, brings in what happens when the outcome does not occur at a single fixed moment but can happen, or fail to happen, at any point over follow-up: survival analysis, censoring, inverse probability of censoring weights, and the clone-censor-weight approach to comparing dynamic treatment strategies, all worked out on the statin cohort, sta. Part IV, Beyond Exchangeability, deals with the harder case in which adjustment for measured covariates is insufficient because of unmeasured confounding, and introduces instrumental variables and sensitivity analysis as ways of asking what an unmeasured confounder would have to look like to explain away a finding.

Threaded through all four parts is a toolkit that you will build yourself, one function at a time. By the end of the book you will have written, tested, and reused standardize() for outcome-model-based standardization, iptw_estimator() for propensity-score weighting, ipcw_estimator() for censoring weights, ccw_estimator() for the clone-censor-weight design, and iv_estimator() for instrumental variable analysis. Each is introduced once, in the chapter where its logic is developed, and then reused in a later exercise on the other cohort, with a different set of covariates, or with a different specification. Writing the functions yourself, rather than only calling a package’s implementation, is meant to make the assumptions each method depends on explicit.

1.7 How to read this book

The chapters are meant to be read with R running alongside them. Every chapter is a self-contained R Markdown file that sources R/setup.R and rebuilds what it needs from there, so you can open any chapter, run it chunk by chunk, and confirm that the numbers in the text are the numbers the code produces. The two risks in the table above, for example, are computed from ns each time this chapter is rendered rather than typed in. When a new function is introduced, read the code closely enough to know what each argument does, then use it on data slightly different from the example in the text before moving to the exercises at the end of each chapter. The exercises themselves mostly do not ask you to write new methods; they ask you to point a function you already wrote at a different variable, a different cohort, or a different specification, and to think about whether the answer it gives still makes sense. That habit of applying a tool you understand to an unfamiliar problem is closer to what causal inference looks like in practice than anything else the book can offer.
