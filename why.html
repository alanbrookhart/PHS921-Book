Causal Inference in Epidemiology Using R

Chapter 1 Why causal inference?

1.1 Questions epidemiology cannot answer with association alone

This book keeps returning to two clinical questions, in different guises, from Chapter 2 onward. Do Cox-2 selective NSAIDs cause less peptic ulcer disease than nonselective NSAIDs? Do statins reduce the risk of a cardiovascular event when they are used the way clinicians actually use them, in routine care rather than in a trial? Both are causal questions, in that they ask what would happen to a group of patients if a particular treatment decision changed, and both are the kind of question epidemiologists are asked to answer using data collected for other purposes: medical records, claims, surveys, registries.

The trouble is that the most natural thing to do with such data, namely compare patients who took a treatment to patients who did not, answers a different and easier question: whether patients who took the treatment did better or worse. Such a comparison yields an association. “Patients who took statins had fewer cardiovascular events” is a statement about a difference in outcomes between two groups of patients as they actually existed. “Statin initiation causes fewer cardiovascular events” is a statement about what would have happened to the same patients under a treatment decision they did not actually make. The two statements can point in the same direction, but nothing about them guarantees it, because the patients who took the treatment and the patients who did not usually differ in the first place, for reasons connected to their risk of the outcome.

Two patterns of difference come up often enough in pharmacoepidemiology to have names. The first is the healthy-user effect: people who reliably take a preventive medication as prescribed also tend to exercise more, smoke less, keep other appointments, and generally do the many small things that predict good health, so a drug looks protective partly because the kind of person who takes it was already going to do well. Statins are a familiar example: some of the early apparent benefit of statin therapy in observational data reflected the fact that adherent, health-conscious patients are also less likely to have a heart attack for reasons unrelated to cholesterol. The second is confounding by indication, sometimes called channeling: physicians assign treatments according to their judgment of which patient needs them, so the treated group differs systematically in exactly the dimension the treatment is meant to address. A Cox-2 selective NSAID is often chosen, deliberately, for a patient a physician already suspects is at higher gastrointestinal risk, so that Cox-2 initiators can appear to fare worse with respect to peptic ulcer disease not because the drug fails to protect them but because they were a higher-risk group at baseline. If the direction of that confounding is misjudged, the apparent effect of a genuinely protective drug can be reversed. A third pattern worth naming here because it will recur throughout the book is prevalent-user bias: comparing people already on a treatment to people never treated mixes in everyone who started the drug, tolerated it, and stayed on it long enough to be captured in the data, while excluding the patients who stopped early because of side effects or lack of benefit. The comparisons in this book are built around new users — patients captured at the moment they start a drug, as ns and sta are — specifically to avoid folding that kind of survivorship into the treatment groups before the analysis begins.

These concerns are not hypothetical. Observational studies of postmenopausal hormone therapy in the 1990s reported that use was associated with substantially lower risk of coronary heart disease; when the Women’s Health Initiative randomized women to hormone therapy or placebo, the trial found an increased risk. The discrepancy did not arise from careless analysis. Rather, women who used hormone therapy were healthier, more health-conscious, and more closely monitored than women who did not, a difference that simple adjustment did not fully remove. If a body of careful nonexperimental research can get the sign of an effect wrong, then a large or richly detailed data set is not by itself sufficient; what is needed is a framework that says when an association can be given a causal interpretation, and what to do when it cannot. The rest of the book develops such a framework.

1.2 A first look at the data

We will spend the whole book with the two cohorts introduced properly in the next chapter, although it is worth looking at the simplest possible comparison in one of them now, before any adjustment, to see what an unadjusted answer to a causal question looks like. In ns, the cohort of new NSAID users, patients either initiated a nonselective NSAID or a Cox-2 selective inhibitor, and the outcome of interest is incident peptic ulcer disease (PUD). The crude risk of PUD in each group follows from a single group_by() and summarise():

risk_tab <- ns |>
  group_by(cox2_initiation) |>
  summarise(n = n(), risk = mean(pud))

risk_tab |>
  knitr::kable(
    col.names = c("Initiated drug", "N", "Risk of incident PUD"),
    digits = c(0, 0, 3)
  )
Initiated drug N Risk of incident PUD
No 2642 0.098
Yes 240 0.125

Among the 2642 patients who initiated a nonselective NSAID, 9.8% went on to develop incident PUD; among the 240 patients who initiated a Cox-2 selective drug, the figure was 12.5%.

Is that comparison an estimate of the causal effect of Cox-2 selectivity on peptic ulcer risk? It is not, and there is reason to think it is far from one. Nothing in the code above accounts for why a given patient started one drug rather than the other. Cox-2 selective inhibitors were developed, and are often chosen, specifically to reduce gastrointestinal risk in patients thought to need that protection. For example, a patient with a history of arthritis, prior GI bleeding, or concurrent corticosteroid or aspirin use is more likely to be steered toward the Cox-2 selective option. If Cox-2 initiators are systematically older or sicker in ways connected to ulcer risk, as we will confirm with a proper Table 1 in the next chapter, then the two rows of this table are not comparing like with like, and the crude risk difference above combines whatever causal effect the drug has with the baseline differences between the groups. Separating those two components, that is, asking what the comparison would look like if the two groups had been alike at baseline, is the problem the rest of the book addresses.

1.3 What this book covers

The book is organized in four parts. Part I, Foundations, which this chapter opens, builds the conceptual vocabulary before any adjustment method is introduced: potential outcomes, the randomized trial as the benchmark against which a causal analysis is implicitly compared, causal diagrams for reasoning about which variables require adjustment, and the elements of study design that turn a causal question into an analyzable data set. Part II, Core Methods, covers the two families of tools most observational analyses reach for first: outcome regression and standardization, and propensity scores, culminating in inverse probability of treatment weighting. Part III, Time, Censoring, and Survival, brings in what happens when the outcome does not occur at a single fixed moment but can happen, or fail to happen, at any point over follow-up: survival analysis, censoring, inverse probability of censoring weights, and the clone-censor-weight approach to comparing dynamic treatment strategies, all worked out on the statin cohort, sta. Part IV, Beyond Exchangeability, deals with the harder case in which adjustment for measured covariates is insufficient because of unmeasured confounding, and introduces instrumental variables and sensitivity analysis as ways of asking what an unmeasured confounder would have to look like to explain away a finding.

Threaded through all four parts is a toolkit that you will build yourself, one function at a time. By the end of the book you will have written, tested, and reused standardize() for outcome-model-based standardization, iptw_estimator() for propensity-score weighting, ipcw_estimator() for censoring weights, ccw_estimator() for the clone-censor-weight design, and iv_estimator() for instrumental variable analysis. Each is introduced once, in the chapter where its logic is developed, and then reused in a later exercise on the other cohort, with a different set of covariates, or with a different specification. Writing the functions yourself, rather than only calling a package’s implementation, is meant to make the assumptions each method depends on explicit.

1.4 How to read this book

The chapters are meant to be read with R running alongside them. Every chapter is a self-contained R Markdown file that sources R/setup.R and rebuilds what it needs from there, so you can open any chapter, run it chunk by chunk, and confirm that the numbers in the text are the numbers the code produces. The two risks in the table above, for example, are computed from ns each time this chapter is rendered rather than typed in. When a new function is introduced, read the code closely enough to know what each argument does, then use it on data slightly different from the example in the text before moving to the exercises at the end of each chapter. The exercises themselves mostly do not ask you to write new methods; they ask you to point a function you already wrote at a different variable, a different cohort, or a different specification, and to think about whether the answer it gives still makes sense. That habit of applying a tool you understand to an unfamiliar problem is closer to what causal inference looks like in practice than anything else the book can offer.
