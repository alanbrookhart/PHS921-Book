Chapter 7 Outcome regression and standardization

Part I built the vocabulary: potential outcomes and estimands in Chapter 3, the target trial in Chapter 4, a diagram and an adjustment set in Chapter 5, and a design that earns the right to compare two arms in Chapter 6. What Part I never did was produce a number. Part II does. Over the next three chapters we answer the question we have been circling since Chapter 1 — does initiating a Cox-2 selective inhibitor, rather than a nonselective NSAID, change the risk of peptic ulcer disease? — three separate times, with three estimators that make the same identification assumptions and reach the same estimand by different routes: standardization here, propensity scores in Chapter 8, and inverse probability of treatment weighting in Chapter 9. They are not rival philosophies but three ways of using the covariates in ns_covs to repair the imbalance Chapter 6’s Table 1 laid out, and part of the point of running all three is to see how little the answer moves when the machinery changes. We also start building the toolkit promised in the preface, with the book’s first estimator function, standardize().

7.1 Adjusting with regression

Let us start where most epidemiologic analyses start, with a regression model for the outcome. Chapter 6’s Table 1 told us that Cox-2 initiators are older, sicker, and more likely to be on corticosteroids than nonselective initiators, so the crude comparison of PUD risk between arms is confounded — and confounded in a direction we can anticipate, since the patients steered toward the Cox-2 drug were steered there partly because their physicians judged them to be at higher GI risk.

ns |>
  group_by(cox2_init) |>
  summarise(n = n(), cases = sum(pud), risk = mean(pud), .groups = "drop") |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
cox2_init n cases risk
0 2642 258 0.098
1 240 30 0.125

The crude risks run 12.5% in the Cox-2 arm against 9.8% among nonselective initiators — a crude risk difference of +2.7 percentage points and a crude risk ratio of 1.28. Taken at face value that says the Cox-2 drug raises ulcer risk, against its entire clinical rationale — the signature of confounding by indication, and the number adjustment has to work on.

The standard first move is a logistic model for pud that includes the treatment indicator alongside every covariate in the adjustment set. Rather than typing nineteen covariate names, we let reformulate() assemble the formula from ns_covs, which keeps R/setup.R as the single source of truth for what “adjusted” means in this book.

library(broom)

fit <- glm(
  reformulate(c("cox2_init", ns_covs), "pud"),
  family = binomial(),
  data = ns
)

tidy(fit, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) |>
  filter(term %in% c("cox2_init", "age", "arthritisYes", "corticosteroid_useYes")) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
term estimate std.error statistic p.value conf.low conf.high
cox2_init 0.929 0.223 -0.331 0.74 0.591 1.418
age 1.066 0.005 13.321 0.00 1.056 1.076
arthritisYes 2.175 0.137 5.655 0.00 1.662 2.849
corticosteroid_useYes 3.202 0.620 1.879 0.06 0.837 10.036

Adjustment does what we hoped. The coefficient on cox2_init, exponentiated, is an odds ratio of 0.93 (95% CI 0.59 to 1.42) — moved from the crude odds ratio of 1.32 to just below the null, though with an interval far too wide to rule out either a modest benefit or a modest harm. Age carries an odds ratio of 1.07 per year, which is where much of the confounding lived.

Now the awkward part. That 0.93 is a conditional odds ratio: the odds ratio comparing two patients who are identical on all nineteen covariates and differ only in which NSAID they started. It is not the marginal contrast we defined in Chapter 3. The ATE was written \(E[Y^1] - E[Y^0]\), an average over the whole population of what would happen under each treatment — a single number attached to a population, not to a stratum. Nothing in a regression coefficient delivers that, and the gap is not merely a matter of rescaling from odds to risks.

The reason is noncollapsibility. If treatment truly has no effect, the conditional and marginal odds ratios agree at 1. Otherwise, even with no confounding at all, the odds ratio averaged over strata is pulled toward the null relative to the odds ratio within any stratum, simply because the odds scale is nonlinear and the strata have different baseline risks. The conditional odds ratio and the marginal odds ratio are answering different questions, and the difference between them is not bias in either — it is a property of the scale. Greenland, Robins, and Pearl (1999) are the standard reference for keeping this distinction straight, and for the point that noncollapsibility and confounding are different phenomena that the same numerical gap can express. Risk differences and risk ratios do not behave this way; they collapse. So the fix is not to choose a better model, it is to stop reading the estimand off a coefficient. We want a marginal contrast, and standardization is how we get one from the very same fitted model.

7.2 Standardization: predict, then average

The recipe, in words, is four steps. Fit an outcome model that includes treatment and the covariates. Then take the entire cohort and set every patient’s treatment to 1 — including the patients who actually took the nonselective drug — and ask the model to predict each patient’s risk in that hypothetical world. Take the whole cohort again, set everyone’s treatment to 0, and predict again. Average each set of predictions and contrast the two averages. This is the parametric g-formula, due to Robins (1986): the model is used only as a device for interpolating what each individual’s risk would have been under each treatment, and the averaging — over the observed covariate distribution of the whole cohort — is what turns those individual predictions into a marginal, population-level contrast.

It takes five lines.

ns1 <- ns |> mutate(cox2_init = 1)
ns0 <- ns |> mutate(cox2_init = 0)
risk1 <- mean(predict(fit, newdata = ns1, type = "response"))
risk0 <- mean(predict(fit, newdata = ns0, type = "response"))
c(risk0 = risk0, risk1 = risk1, rd = risk1 - risk0, rr = risk1 / risk0)
##        risk0        risk1           rd           rr 
##  0.100531704  0.095016560 -0.005515145  0.945140244

Notice what the two copies of the data are doing. ns1 is the cohort as it would look if every one of these 2882 patients had started a Cox-2 inhibitor, with every covariate left exactly as observed; ns0 is the same cohort under the nonselective strategy. Because both averages are taken over the same covariate distribution — everybody, once each, in both worlds — the difference between them cannot be attributed to a difference in case mix. That is the whole trick, and it is the same trick as classical direct standardization to a reference population, with the fitted model standing in for the stratum-specific rates.

Every step of that reasoning leans on Chapter 3’s identification conditions. Consistency lets us read a predicted risk under cox2_init = 1 as a potential outcome; conditional exchangeability, which Chapter 5’s diagram is our argument for, is what makes a model fit partly to treated patients usable for predicting untreated ones. And positivity is what keeps the prediction honest: with only 240 Cox-2 initiators among 2882 patients, the model extrapolates fairly hard when it imagines a Cox-2 world for covariate patterns where almost no Cox-2 initiator was observed. Standardization will produce a number anyway, without complaining — a reason to keep Chapter 6’s Table 1 in view rather than trusting the estimator blindly.

We are going to want this in Chapters 8 and 9, so let us package it. The function takes the data, a model formula, and the name of the treatment column, and returns one row per estimand. Two details are worth pausing on. The "{treat}" := 1 syntax inside mutate() is dplyr’s glue notation for assigning to a column whose name is held in a variable, which is what lets the same function work for cox2_init here and statin later. And the point estimates are wrapped in their own inner function, point_estimates(), because the bootstrap in the next section needs to run exactly the same computation on a resampled data set — fitting the model inside the resample, not reusing the original fit.

standardize <- function(data, outcome_model, treat, family = binomial(),
                        n_boot = 200, conf_level = 0.95, seed = NULL) {
  if (!is.null(seed)) set.seed(seed)

  point_estimates <- function(d) {
    fit <- glm(outcome_model, family = family, data = d)
    d1 <- d |> mutate("{treat}" := 1)
    d0 <- d |> mutate("{treat}" := 0)
    risk1 <- mean(predict(fit, newdata = d1, type = "response"))
    risk0 <- mean(predict(fit, newdata = d0, type = "response"))
    c(risk0 = risk0, risk1 = risk1, rd = risk1 - risk0, rr = risk1 / risk0)
  }

  est <- point_estimates(data)
  boot_est <- replicate(n_boot, {
    point_estimates(slice_sample(data, prop = 1, replace = TRUE))
  })
  alpha <- 1 - conf_level

  tibble(
    estimand  = names(est),
    estimate  = unname(est),
    conf_low  = apply(boot_est, 1, quantile, probs = alpha / 2),
    conf_high = apply(boot_est, 1, quantile, probs = 1 - alpha / 2)
  )
}

That code lives in R/standardize.R, so from here on we source it rather than retype it.

source("R/standardize.R")
std <- standardize(
  ns,
  outcome_model = reformulate(c("cox2_init", ns_covs), "pud"),
  treat = "cox2_init", n_boot = 200, seed = 921
)
std |> knitr::kable(digits = 3)
estimand estimate conf_low conf_high
risk0 0.101 0.090 0.112
risk1 0.095 0.064 0.124
rd -0.006 -0.038 0.025
rr 0.945 0.624 1.240

Here is the answer, on the scale Chapter 3 asked for. Had every patient in ns started a nonselective NSAID, the model puts PUD risk at 10.1%; had every one of them started a Cox-2 inhibitor, 9.5%. The standardized risk difference is -0.6 percentage points (95% CI -3.8 to +2.5) and the standardized risk ratio 0.95 (0.62 to 1.24). Adjustment has moved the crude risk ratio of 1.28 to 0.95 — from apparent harm to a point estimate slightly on the protective side of the null — but the interval comfortably includes 1, so what the data support is “no large effect either way,” not “a benefit.” With 288 ulcer cases and 240 Cox-2 initiators, that is about as much precision as this cohort has to offer.

Note also what noncollapsibility looks like in the arithmetic. The standardized risks imply a marginal odds ratio of 0.939, while the regression coefficient gave a conditional odds ratio of 0.929 — the conditional one sitting slightly further from the null, exactly as predicted. The gap is small here only because PUD is uncommon; with a common outcome it can be substantial.

7.3 The bootstrap

The point estimates came out of a glm() fit, so it is tempting to reach for that model’s standard errors. They do not apply. The standardized risk difference is not a parameter of the logistic model at all — it is a nonlinear function of every coefficient in the model, combined with the empirical covariate distribution of the cohort. There is no line in the summary() output whose standard error describes it, and the delta-method calculation that would produce one by hand is tedious and specification-specific.

Resampling sidesteps the algebra. The logic is this: the sampling variability of our estimate comes from the fact that ns is one draw of 2882 patients from some larger population, so we mimic that draw by sampling 2882 patients from ns with replacement, and recompute the whole estimator — refit the model, re-predict both worlds, re-average, re-contrast — on each resample. The spread of those recomputed estimates approximates the spread we would see across repeated samples from the population, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of that spread give a 95% percentile confidence interval. The critical discipline is that everything downstream of sampling must be redone inside each replicate, model fitting included; bootstrapping only the averaging step, with the coefficients held fixed, would ignore the largest source of uncertainty in the whole procedure.

n_boot trades precision for time. Each replicate is a full glm() fit on 2882 rows, and our default of 200 is enough to place an interval roughly while keeping a book that re-renders often tolerable to build. The percentile endpoints are themselves noisy at that size, though, so a different seed will shift them in the second decimal place; for a result you intend to publish, 1,000 or 2,000 is the right order of magnitude, and seed is what makes whichever number you choose reproducible.

7.4 Effect modification

So far we have estimated one effect for everybody. But the effect of a Cox-2 inhibitor might plausibly differ by whether a patient is also taking aspirin, since aspirin damages gastric mucosa through the same prostaglandin pathway the Cox-2 drug was designed to spare. When a causal effect differs across levels of a covariate, we call that effect modification (or heterogeneity of treatment effect), and we look for it by letting the treatment coefficient depend on that covariate — an interaction term.

fit_int <- glm(
  reformulate(c("cox2_init", ns_covs, "cox2_init:aspirin_use"), "pud"),
  family = binomial(), data = ns
)

tidy(fit_int, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) |>
  filter(str_detect(term, "cox2_init")) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
term estimate std.error statistic p.value conf.low conf.high
cox2_init 0.936 0.228 -0.291 0.771 0.588 1.443
cox2_init:aspirin_useYes 0.870 0.995 -0.140 0.889 0.098 5.480

The interaction term is a ratio of odds ratios, 0.87 with a p-value of 0.89 — no evidence of modification, which is unsurprising given that only 92 of the 2882 patients use aspirin and only 9 of those started a Cox-2 inhibitor. But the coefficient is not what we want anyway, for the same reason as before: it is a conditional, noncollapsible quantity on the odds scale. The subgroup effects we actually care about are two standardized risk differences, one per aspirin stratum.

Standardizing within a subgroup means changing the population we average over: instead of predicting both worlds for the whole cohort, we predict both worlds for the aspirin users only, then repeat for the non-users. Fitting a separate model within each stratum is the fully flexible version of the interaction above — it lets every coefficient, not just treatment’s, differ by aspirin use — so we can get subgroup effects by calling standardize() on each subset. One adjustment is forced on us: aspirin_use is constant within a stratum by construction and has to leave the covariate list, and among aspirin users anti_coagulant_use turns out to be constant too. With only 92 aspirin users we cannot support nineteen covariates in that stratum in any case. We fit a deliberately lean model in both strata so the two are comparable.

lean <- reformulate(c("cox2_init", "age", "sex", "arthritis"), "pud")

sub <- bind_rows(
  standardize(filter(ns, aspirin_use == "No"),  lean, "cox2_init", seed = 922) |>
    mutate(aspirin = "No", .before = 1),
  standardize(filter(ns, aspirin_use == "Yes"), lean, "cox2_init", seed = 923) |>
    mutate(aspirin = "Yes", .before = 1)
)
sub |> knitr::kable(digits = 3)
aspirin estimand estimate conf_low conf_high
No risk0 0.098 0.087 0.108
No risk1 0.091 0.061 0.120
No rd -0.007 -0.040 0.021
No rr 0.930 0.609 1.214
Yes risk0 0.196 0.117 0.276
Yes risk1 0.193 0.000 0.353
Yes rd -0.003 -0.204 0.165
Yes rr 0.986 0.000 2.048

Aspirin users start from a much higher baseline: a standardized nonselective-NSAID risk of 19.6% against 9.8% among non-users, which is the pathway acting as advertised. The standardized risk differences, though, are -0.3 points among aspirin users and -0.7 points among non-users, and the aspirin-user interval runs from -20.4 to +16.5 percentage points — wide enough to contain nearly any clinically meaningful effect in either direction. The risk ratio row is worse still, with a lower bound at essentially zero: in some resamples the handful of aspirin-using Cox-2 initiators produced a predicted risk near zero, and the bootstrap reports that instability faithfully. It is a signal about the data, not a discovery about the drug. We suppressed R’s separation warnings in that chunk to keep the output readable, but they tell the same story.

It is worth being precise about how effect modification differs from confounding, because both get described loosely as “a covariate that matters.” Confounding is a problem of identification: a common cause of treatment and outcome makes the observed contrast fail to equal the causal one, and we adjust for it to recover a valid estimate. Effect modification is not a problem at all — it is a feature of the causal effect itself. A confounder must be handled or the answer is wrong; an effect modifier is something we choose to report on, and the estimand changes when we do, from one ATE for the cohort to a set of subgroup-specific ATEs. A variable can be one, the other, or both: aspirin use here is a candidate for either role. Note also that the overall standardized risk difference of -0.6 points is a weighted average of the subgroup effects, weighted by how common each subgroup is — which is why a cohort-level ATE can be small while an effect in a small subgroup is large, and why the population you standardize to is part of the estimand, not an implementation detail.

7.5 Exercises

  1. The model above enters age linearly on the log-odds scale. Refit it with poly(age, 2) in place of age and rerun standardize() with the same seed. How much do the standardized risks and the risk difference move? Does the confidence interval change enough to alter what you would conclude, and what does your answer suggest about how sensitive this estimate is to the functional form of the strongest confounder?
  2. ppi_use and h2_antagonist_use are gastroprotective drugs, so they may be markers of a physician’s concern about GI risk rather than causes of it. Drop both from the covariate list and rerun standardize(). Report the standardized risk difference with and without them. This is an informal sensitivity analysis to the choice of adjustment set; using Chapter 5’s language, sketch the DAG under which including them is right and the DAG under which it is wrong.
  3. Everything in this chapter estimated the ATE, averaging predictions over the whole cohort. The ATT averages over the treated only. Modify point_estimates() inside a copy of standardize() so that the two predicted risks are averaged over filter(d, cox2_init == 1) while the model is still fit on all of d, and estimate the ATT for the Cox-2 question. Why must the model still be fit on the full data even though the averaging is restricted, and would you expect the ATT here to sit above or below the ATE given Chapter 6’s Table 1?
